# The worlds

Ten worlds built out in full — geography, peoples, cultures, relations, history. Generated by a
multi-agent pass and then differentiated against a shared ledger; see the known issues at the foot
of this file before treating any of it as settled.

`world.md` holds the setting and the peoples. This holds the places.

**The method:** geography first, culture second. Each people is seeded from a real human culture's
*structure* — how obligation, tenure, apprenticeship and debt actually work — and then pushed
somewhere that culture never went by this world's terrain.

## Orrowan V

*ordinary*

A cold basalt shelf, a cloud-forest scarp, a delta that reworks itself every forty years, and nine hundred kilometres of fogged coast with no trees on it. Tomás Iriarte comes off the delta and Ekene Baptiste off the shelf, which on Orrowan V means their families have been doing business with each other for six hundred years.

### Geography

One continent and a long drowned shelf offshore. Two barriers do most of the explaining.

The interior is **the Kalva**: basalt at three thousand metres, cold, dry and hard-lit, where nothing grows except along the **draws** — ribbons of warm ground, forty metres wide and sometimes forty kilometres long, where a ley current surfaces along a fracture and condensate collects. Everyone on the Kalva lives strung out along a draw. Draws also wander, sealing in one place and opening in another over sixty or eighty years.

The shelf's eastern edge falls thirteen hundred metres in fifteen kilometres. That is **the Aleth**: permanent cloud, permanent wet, the least stable ground on the planet, and four passes through it that exist only because they are worked.

Below it, warm and flooded, is **the Barrena Spread** — a delta the size of a small sea. The river moves its main flow tens of kilometres every thirty to fifty years. Two hundred square kilometres goes under. Other ground comes up.

West of the Kalva the shelf ramps down in stages to **the Marsk**: cold coast under a cold current, fog most days, gravel bars and salt marsh in the north, cliff in the south, and no timber at all above water. What there is grows offshore on the drowned shelf and has to be cut underwater. The current sets north the whole length of the coast, so anything lost anywhere on the Marsk comes ashore on the northern bars, and everyone has always known roughly where.

### Peoples (4)

#### The Ereta

**Mostly human. A large amphibioid population through the lower channels, and a sunfed minority in the upper Spread.** · The Barrena Spread — the eastern lowland delta. Warm, seasonally flooded, and not in the same shape it was in a century ago.

*Seed: Char-land tenure of the Brahmaputra–Jamuna: an inherited claim to ground that is currently under water, made good by re-occupation when the river gives it back. The terrain push is that Orrowan's river moves further and stays longer, so re-occupation is not enough — the claim has to be alive in the meantime, somewhere else, in the hands of somebody else's family.*

An Ereta line holds a parcel, and the parcel is often not there. The river moves; a claim does not. What makes a claim good is not a document but a graft — when ground surfaces, the line brings its stock, a living cutting descended from the tree that stood there, and splices it onto whatever pioneered the new silt first. The graft takes or it does not. That is the whole of the law.

So every Ereta learns to graft at about six, the way other people learn to swim, and nobody regards it as a trade. A cook can splice living wood. Keeping stock alive is the household's serious work, and stock outlives the people tending it; there are cuttings in the Spread with four hundred years of continuous care behind them and nothing yet to show for it.

You do not nail anything together in a delta. You plant piles and let them root, and a house is a thing that is still growing.

**Look.** Sap. Hands and forearms stain amber and never come clean, and how far up it goes tells you how much grafting somebody does. Nothing is cut below the knee and nothing fastens with a button, because both are misery in waist-deep water — everything ties. A hook-knife on a lanyard, always.

And the root-wrap: a clay-and-cloth bundle for keeping a cutting damp, carried everywhere and worked over by generations of idle hands. A plain new wrap means a family whose ground is up. A beautiful one does not.

**Names.** Given name plus the name of the parcel the line holds, whether or not the parcel is currently above water. Tomás Iriarte, Izarne Lezama, Anton Ondarre, Domeka Uztaro, Sabas Etxarte, Nekane Goikoa.

#### The Kalvec

**Humans, mana dwellers in real numbers, and oni — the oni do the fracture prospecting, because a horn feels a seam closing about a decade before the ground does, and it is unglamorous paid work rather than a gift.** · The Kalva — the high shelf. Cold, dry, thin, and habitable in ribbons.

*Seed: Omani *falaj* water shares: what you own is an interval of time in a flowing channel, measured, inherited, divided and traded entirely separately from land. The terrain push is that on the Kalva the channel itself relocates over decades, so the shares routinely outlive the thing they are shares in, and a holding can be a perfectly valid claim on nothing at all.*

A draw has a beat — the current pulses, and you can count it — and what a Kalvec household owns is a number of beats at a named draw. Not the ground, not the water, not the warmth. The turn. Shares are inherited, split, sold and married, and a holding is a schedule rather than a place. Draws seal and reopen elsewhere and the shares outlive the draw, so families still attend the yearly reading at ground that has been cold for sixty years.

Somebody calls the rotation. It is unpaid and exacting, and whoever holds it takes their own turn last. That is not a courtesy. A caller standing anywhere else in the order has an interest in the count, and a count with an interest in it is not a count. Custody accumulates the way shares do, and a caller under fifty is a scandal or an emergency.

The other trade is keeping. Warm ground on a draw is the best nursery on the planet, and for six hundred years Kalvec households have raised Ereta line-stock under a share of beats, for a fee, without once failing.

**Look.** Thin air and hard sun at three thousand metres. Almost every Kalvec carries pale scarring across the nose and cheekbones from childhood and none of them thinks it is a mark of anything. Layers, with the outer one undyed — dye does not survive up there, so the outside of a Kalvec is deliberately the cheap part.

And the weight: a small plumb on a knotted cord, for counting beats you cannot hear. Children are given one. The mana-deaf need it and the attuned carry it anyway, because you cannot settle a schedule by ear in front of witnesses.

Mana dwellers on the draws are common enough that nobody remarks on it. One raised over a surfacing current reads low for the rest of their life once they leave one, and will give you the figure rather than say they are cold.

**Names.** A personal name plus the name of the ancestor the shares count from. A household holding shares from two directions carries both, so a long name is a large holding and everyone in the room hears it arrive. Ekene Baptiste, Odessa Nnamdi, Fabrice Odile, Solange Chidi, Marguerite Nnamdi Baptiste.

#### The Alethen

**Humans, bird folk, and rock skins. The rock skins read slopes, which on a scarp that slides is the senior work.** · The Aleth — the scarp between shelf and delta. Thirteen hundred metres, four passes, and cloud most of the year.

*Seed: Alpine impartible inheritance — *Anerbenrecht*, where one heir takes the holding undivided and the rest are settled with and go. The terrain push is that what cannot be divided here is not a farm but a length of route, so there is no smallholding to fall back to and no marginal land to clear: the two available directions are down and up, and they have been for six thousand years.*

What an Alethen household holds is a section — a named stretch of route through a forest that grows back over it in one season and slides in a bad year. The routes exist because people live on them and work them, and they stop existing about four years after somebody stops.

A section cannot be divided, because half a route is not a route. One child takes it. The others leave at about sixteen, down into the Spread or up onto the Kalva, and that is the arithmetic.

It is not a tragedy and they get irritated when outsiders make it one. There is a proper leaving, there are people at the other end expecting you, and the ones who go mostly do well. What there is not is a way back, because there is no second section.

Everyone born to a section carries its name for life, heir or not. Two Alethen meeting anywhere ask the section first and the pass second.

**Look.** Wet. Permanently — the Aleth is in cloud most of the year and Alethen dress for it in oiled hooded overlayers, hood up indoors, which lowlanders read as standoffish and which is just what a hood does after twenty years. Rope callus across both palms, including the people who do not work rope.

Scarp rock skins band a particular dark green-grey off the basalt and the moss, and Alethen abroad spot one another by a forearm across a concourse. Bird folk working the slide zones come back red to the knee with slip-clay, and it does not wash out for a fortnight.

**Names.** Given name plus the section, carried for life by every child of the house and not only by the heir — so most Alethen names abroad belong to a stretch of route the bearer will never work. Miren Sereth, Ansel Loure, Teve Cavessa, Robene Mella, Cass Odren, Odalie Tannic.

#### The Marsk

**Humans, amphibioids in large numbers, and rock skins. The amphibioids cut the timber, which is underwater, which is why they crew off every bight on the coast. The rock skins work the winter bars in water that would put anybody else in a suit.** · The Marsk — the western coast. Cold current setting north, fog, gravel bars and salt marsh in the north, cliff in the south, drowned forest offshore.

*Seed: Cornish and Breton wreck custom: the strand owns what the sea brings, not the finder — the holder of the foreshore takes the goods, the salvor takes a fraction fixed by custom rather than negotiated, and anything carrying an owner's mark is not wreck at all until a set period has run. The terrain push is that the current here is reliable and the drowned forest sheds constantly, so drift is not a windfall but an income, which makes the fractions the most important numbers on the coast and makes marking a thing the first act of owning it.*

A Marsk household holds a bight — a named stretch of the northern bars, or a cliff foot in the south — and everything the current lays on it. Three things are fixed and never argued: life comes off the water before goods; whoever takes a thing off the water keeps a third; and anything bearing a mark waits a year and a day on the rack above the tide line before it is anyone's. So a Marsk marks a thing the day they get it. Unmarked is not modest. Unmarked is abandoned.

The south cuts and the north catches. Cliff bights sit over the steepest of the drowned forest and take holdfast at the stump; the bars take whatever the sea tears loose and carries up. The third moves between them all year, which is why nobody works only their own water and why a boat is crewed off four or five bights at once.

A boat is not property. It is a standing agreement between bights, laid down out of pooled thirds, and it carries a name. Lose it and the bights decide whether to lay another keel under it. Sometimes they do not. Nobody is cruel about that, and everybody can do the arithmetic.

**Look.** Oiled, hooded, heavy, and the same weight of clothing in every season, because you are wet in all of them. Hands are the tell: cold water, wet rope and holdfast wreck a Marsk's hands by thirty and nobody is self-conscious about it. Amphibioid Marsk have the same hands, and forearms banded pale from years in the shelf water.

Nobody wears a bight mark. The mark goes on the gear — burned into the boat, the tools, the oar looms, and, aboard ship, the tea mug. A Marsk will carry a borrowed spanner back across three decks rather than set it down somewhere it could be mistaken for drift.

**Names.** Given name and boat name, spoken with 'of'. Havie of *Orlen*, Tove of *Semme*, Brann of *Ruen*, Nellin of *Brack*, Orsk of *Halde*, Maike of *Cove*. Lay a new keel under the name and the name is unchanged, because the name belongs to the agreement and not to the wood.

### How they regard each other

**The Kalvec, the Ereta and the Alethen** — *a three-way entanglement — no two of them are the unit*

The Kalvec have cut catchment berms for four thousand years, which is the only reason anybody lives on the shelf, and they hold back about a third of what used to come over the Aleth. Ground in the Spread that used to resurface in thirty years now takes eighty. But the berms are stone carried up the Aleth passes, and the passes stay open only because Ereta stock goes up and Kalvec keeping brings it back down, all year, every year. So every fix is a three-hander. Twice the Ereta and the Kalvec have agreed terms the Alethen would have had to carry uphill for nothing, and both agreements died on the scarp. They argue about the keeping fee instead, because a fee is the one number two of them can move alone.

**The Alethen and the Ereta** — *old grievance, entirely one-sided, and the wrong side is the one apologising*

One child keeps the section and the rest come down, and they have been coming down for six thousand years, so something close to a quarter of Ereta lines were founded by somebody's second daughter off the Aleth. The Ereta do not experience this as mixing. They experience Alethen as a sort of upstream Ereta who talk oddly and dress for rain, and they say so warmly, at length, to Alethen faces. The Alethen have explained that they are a different people with a different inheritance. They have explained it repeatedly, to people who nod, agree, and carry on.

**The Marsk and the Ereta** — *improving fast, and neither of them planned it*

For six centuries this was a three-week sail round the southern cape, twice a year: salt, oil and cut holdfast east, grafting stock and dried fruit west. Then the Barrena avulsed nine years ago and put its main mouth eighty kilometres south. The run is eleven days now. Five crossings a year, and two peoples who liked each other on almost no evidence are finding out what the other is actually like. Mostly they are pleased. The gift problem has got considerably worse — an Ereta host hands you things constantly and means nothing by it, a Marsk cannot take anything without naming the day they will return it, and that used to be an annual curiosity rather than a weekly one.

**The Kalvec and the Marsk** — *thinning, for a reason neither of them has worked out*

The western ramp is gentle and the two have walked up and down it without incident for as long as there is anything to say about. A Marsk understands a share in beats the first time it is explained; a Kalvec reads a bight roll at a glance. But the boats that used to winter at the foot of the ramp winter south now, because the delta run is eleven days and worth making twice more a season, and the ramp market has gone from four gatherings a year to two. Nobody decided this. Every bight made a sensible decision on its own. The Kalvec who grew up walking down to it are irritated and have nobody to be irritated at.

**The Alethen and the Marsk** — *no relation at all, and neither has ever got round to inventing one*

The Aleth is on the eastern edge of the shelf, the Marsk is on the western coast, and there are eleven hundred kilometres of basalt in between with nothing on it either of them wants. Neither has a word for the other's country. Put one of each aboard a ship, though, and they are friends inside a fortnight — two hooded, rope-callused, permanently damp peoples with no history to be careful about — and both find this faintly embarrassing, because it looks like something old and is not. The Ereta find it hilarious and say so.

### History

The Kalva was settled from the west, up the ramp, not from the Spread — the Aleth is a harder barrier than nine hundred kilometres of coast. The first catchment berms are four thousand years old and nobody invented them. Each generation added a few and the shelf slowly became a place people could live.

The passes are older. The Aleth routes have been worked continuously for longer than anything else on the planet has been worked continuously, which is the Alethen's one loud claim and is probably true.

Three hundred and eighty years ago the Barrena went into the Losoa channel and stayed there — the largest avulsion in the record: nine hundred square kilometres under water, forty named parcels gone in a season, Iriarte among them. That is when Ereta lines began sending stock up the passes in numbers, and when Kalvec keeping went from a favour to a trade with a price on it. Neither has stopped since.

The Marsk keep strand rolls back about six hundred years — every marked thing that came ashore, who took it off the water, and whether it was claimed inside the year. In one decade in the middle of them eleven boats went and four names were never laid down again. The rolls say so plainly, with the names, and that is the closest the Marsk come to a monument.

The Census surveyed Orrowan V two centuries ago and nothing dramatic followed. The draws supply a small, dull mana industry; the pass markets have an orbital.

### The Null

Untouched. Three lanes run to Orrowan V and all three sing, which makes it one of the few places on the crew list a person could in principle go back to.

What that comes to, in practice, is one hour of Tomás Iriarte's week. Post moves both ways when the lanes are good, so the parcels other crew hand him actually arrive, and a parcel that arrives gets answered, and an answer has to be answered back. He has kept that hour for eleven years, writing to families he has never met on behalf of people who have not got round to it.

Half of it is not post at all. He has a survey chart above the desk, two hundred years old, and people on Orrowan write to him because he is the man aboard with the chart. The Barrena avulsed nine years ago. The news took four years to reach the ship; he spent a week working out whose ground had come up; about sixty parcels had. He gives people the number cheerfully. Iriarte was not one of them and he has never put that in a letter.

Ekene Baptiste is Kalvec, off a draw that kept a dozen Ereta wraps, and would have the whole of it in about four seconds. Nobody has ever put the two of them in a room about it.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- Talks continuously while working, at ordinary volume, about whatever is in front of them. On a flood line you keep talking so the rest of the line knows where you are and that you are still there. It stops being a safety habit at about twelve and never stops being a habit.
- Keeps something alive that has no purpose — a cutting in a clay-and-cloth wrap, root-damp, carried from berth to berth and watered on a schedule. They will not explain it and would not know where to begin. The same person can graft, because every Ereta can, learned at six the way other people learn to swim, and does not think of it as a skill.
- Takes their own turn last. A Kalvec who ends up running any rota at all will put themselves at the bottom of it without noticing, and is confused when thanked, because a count is only worth anything from down there.
- Distrusts a shared thing with no published order of turns. A Kalvec looks at a galley that has never had a rota and sees somebody quietly taking two, and cannot be argued out of it by evidence. This costs every Kalvec aboard one very awkward first month.
- Marks it the day they get it. A Marsk burns or scratches their sign into a mug, a torch, a pair of boots and a borrowed spanner, because at home an unmarked thing on the water belongs to whoever lifts it. They will carry a borrowed tool back across three decks rather than set it down where somebody might reasonably take it for going spare.
- Cannot walk past a blocked way. An Alethen clears a gallery of whatever has been left in it, on their own time, without mentioning it and without being thanked, and gets short with people who step over things.

### Comedy

Kalvec keepers name the Ereta trees they tend. Not formally — affectionately, and usually badly, and the name outlives whoever coined it. So an Ereta arriving at a draw to see four centuries of their family's line-stock for the first time gets introduced to it as Bother, by a fifteen-year-old who has loved it since she could walk and who is using her grandmother's word for it, and has to find a face to make. Everyone involved finds this excruciating. Nobody has ever suggested stopping.

The other one is Tomás. An Ereta surname is a parcel, so an Ereta hears every name as a place, and Tomás Iriarte has asked half this crew about their drainage. He did it to Aurelio Bassi, who is third-generation shipborn and has never been anywhere, and the pause went on long enough that Tomás started explaining the delta.

## Sindal II

*ordinary*

Two ley strands braid over one featureless supercontinent, and where they cross they beat against each other; everything on Sindal II is built around that, and around the fact that the braid will not stay still. Three peoples answer the same moving sky three incompatible ways — spread yourself across it, walk with it, or stay exactly where you are and re-let the sky over your head — and nothing separating them can be pointed at on a map.

### Geography

Sindal II has almost no shape. One low supercontinent — **Ollun**, nine thousand kilometres across, averaging a hundred and eighty metres above the sea — and one shallow inland sea, the **Ilme**, nowhere deeper than thirty. The highest ground on the planet is a volcanic stub of 610 metres and people travel to look at it. Nothing down here stops anybody.

The divisions are overhead. Two ley strands braid across Ollun. The **Ioma** runs to growth and decay: grain ripens in forty days and moulds in nine, timber goes soft in a season, nothing keeps. The **Dessek** runs to order and energy: a thing tuned under Dessek holds tune for years, and nothing rots, including what you would like to. Where the two cross they beat against each other — the **Suhr**, one slow pulse every ninety seconds, detuning everything it touches, and at the top of each pulse about eleven seconds of a push available nowhere else on the planet.

And the braid slides. How it slides where you stand is the only geography that matters. In the east the crossings sit a hundred kilometres apart and the whole pattern walks eight a year. In the west the strands run parallel, six hundred kilometres apart, drifting under one, with dead calm between them — the **Aske**. In the north, one arm alone, no crossings at all, swinging its full width across sixty years.

Climate exists — monsoon on the east coast, dry west of the Ilme, ice in the south. It explains nothing. There are fisher towns all round the Ilme and people under the southern ice with arrangements of their own.

### Peoples (3)

#### The Kavren

**Mixed. Mana dwellers are almost absent — the Suhr is not survivable for a body that is partly current, and a household that cannot work Suhr ground is finished — so the Kavren are the one people on Sindal who mostly meet mana dwellers off-world and are awkward about it. Tesk are heavily over-represented: eleven-second work inside a tight lattice is Tesk work. Oni are common and never on the Suhr rotation, because a conductive horn takes the beat every ninety seconds, and the household simply arranges around it. Also humans, canids, and sunfed, who do extraordinarily well on Ioma ground.** · The eastern braid — crossings a hundred kilometres apart, the whole pattern sliding six to nine kilometres a year, so every current is within three days' walk and none of them stays put

*Seed: Andean vertical-archipelago tenure: one kin group holding non-contiguous parcels in several ecological tiers at once — high, middle and low — each staffed by resident relatives, produce moving inward, the map a mosaic with no clean territory anywhere. The structural element is simultaneous holdings across incompatible zones, worked by your own people rather than traded for. Sindal swapped altitude for current and then made the tiers move.*

A Kavren household is a triangle. One holding under Ioma, which feeds it. One under Dessek, which keeps and makes. One on the Suhr, which does the work nothing else can do. Nobody lives at the centre because there is no centre — people move between the three on a standing rotation, and the rotation is what the household actually is.

Then the braid slides eight kilometres a year, a Dessek holding becomes Suhr ground, and a household with two Suhr holdings and no Dessek is **out of set**. So the work is not farming. It is balancing: acquiring, shedding, leasing, walking out to look at ground nobody is using yet. The person who keeps a household in set is the one everybody defers to, and they are frequently neither the eldest nor, strictly, in the household.

A household is a body you join, not a line you are born into. Children start in their mother's and about a fifth move to another by twenty, by agreement, without anybody making a scene. Being out of set is not poverty. It is exposure, everyone can see it, and turning up is ordinary.

**Look.** Dressed to be somewhere else by Thursday. Everything rolls, everything is on a strap, and nobody owns furniture — furniture belongs to the holding and whoever is standing in it. Most Kavren carry three beads on a cord at the belt and work them in the fingers all day without looking. They are sunburnt on one forearm and not the other, which is a rotation joke, and true.

**Names.** A given name and the current you were born under: **Ioman**, **Desseki**, **Suhren**. Because a household spans all three and women work the rotation while pregnant, siblings routinely carry different surnames, and a Kavren surname therefore tells you nothing whatever about family and everything about where their mother was that year. Kavren find this unremarkable and everyone else finds it maddening. Given names: **Menna, Aro, Sevi, Tuli, Orem, Kade** — so, Menna Suhren and Aro Ioman, who are brother and sister.

#### The Yurrel

**Mixed, and the distribution is entirely practical. Canids are everywhere, because a canid smells the arm's edge about two days before it arrives and nothing else the Yurrel own does better. Bird folk scout the open north. Sunfed are numerous and are the one real problem: a sunfed who goes dormant during a fast shift has to be carried, sometimes for eleven days, and the carrying is a rota rather than a kindness and is not discussed. Very few mana dwellers — the arm's middle is a medical event and the Yurrel live in it.** · North of the Ilme, under the single wandering Ioma arm — no crossings, no Suhr, and the whole arm swinging its width across eleven hundred kilometres in sixty years

*Seed: Mongolian *otor*: the customary right to move stock onto another's pasture in extremity, which the holder cannot refuse, bounded by convention as to how long, what may be taken, and what must not be exhausted. The structural element is an enforceable right of entry *claimed by the guest* rather than shelter offered by the host. The arm's stall-and-jump gave it a trigger nobody on the steppe needed.*

The arm does not drift. It stalls for forty years and then goes two hundred kilometres in a season, and since nothing keeps under Ioma there is nothing stored to sit out the gap. So the Yurrel walk, and when a jump catches them on dead ground they claim the **walking right**: entry onto Kavren or Weyn ground, which the holder may not refuse.

It is claimed, not asked, and that distinction is the whole of it. The limits are old and everybody knows them. It runs until the arm is back within sixty days' walk. You may take standing growth and not seed and not stock. You leave the ground worked — cleared, dug out, mended — and the work is not payment, and calling it payment is the insult. Eleven claims in four hundred years, between two and nine years each.

A Yurrel does not thank you. Off-world this costs them friends for about a decade each, and every Yurrel finds out eventually, and most of them are horrified.

**Look.** Everything they own is worn or on cord, because pockets lose things. Hands scarred across the backs from cutting green growth at speed. A Yurrel entering a room glances at the ceiling and both doors inside about four seconds — reading it as a camp — and then looks you straight in the face for the rest of the conversation, which people find either very direct or very restful.

**Names.** Everyone is **Ioman**. The whole people, one surname, because they follow one strand and always have — so the convention that does real work for the other two tells a Yurrel nothing, and among themselves they add the number of the arm-shift they were born under. The count stands at fifty-one. Given names: **Nevi, Hala, Bosk, Imre, Ruthe, Anu** — so, Nevi Ioman, forty-ninth. Written out on a crew form as *Nevi Ioman 49*, which has been corrected to a date by well-meaning people four times.

#### The Weyn

**Mixed. Mana dwellers are common in the Aske — about one household in twelve, against one in several hundred anywhere else on Sindal — because the dead calm between the strands is the only ground on the planet that does not hurt them, and they have been there a very long time. Rock skins in the stone country, oni along the steady Dessek margin, amphibioids all down the Ilme shore, humans throughout. Nobody thinks the Aske is a mana dweller place. It simply is one.** · The western plain — an Ioma band on one edge, a Dessek band six hundred kilometres away on the other, and four hundred kilometres of dead calm, the Aske, in between, the whole arrangement drifting under a kilometre a year

*Seed: Yemeni qat-terrace usufruct: title and use as two separate heritable estates over the same ground, the usufruct let out on long terms, the maintenance obligation — terrace walls, channels — attached to the usufructuary rather than the owner, and water taken in a fixed turn that belongs to the land and not the holder. The structural element is use split from ownership with upkeep as the rent. Here what is let is not the soil. It is the sky.*

Ground and current are two estates, and a Weyn family holds the ground for a thousand years. Title never moves. The **draw** — the right to take what runs over that ground — is let separately, usually for ninety years, and whoever holds a draw owes the works: the set stone, the tuning rings, the long low courses that steady a current over a field. Upkeep is the rent. It is inspected by the family whose ground it is, which is the only day in the year the two estates are in the same place, and it is attended very closely by both.

Out in the Aske there is nothing to hold, so what little runs is taken in turn, and the turn belongs to the ground. Nobody has altered a turn in three hundred years. People have moved house to get one.

Then the band creeps and a village crosses out of Dessek into the Aske, and everybody in it takes the new name on the same day. Nine times in four hundred years.

**Look.** Built for dry heat and stone: light, covered, and dusted to the elbow. They are the only people on Sindal with old buildings, and it shows in how they stand in them. Most Weyn carry a short iron rule, three fingers long, and use it on other people's stonework, joins and door frames without asking first, and are usually apologetic and never quite sorry.

**Names.** A given name and the current overhead — **Desseki**, **Ioman**, or **Asken** for the Aske. Uniquely, a Weyn's name is a claim about now rather than about birth, so a Weyn changes it when the band crosses them, without going anywhere, at the age of fifty-odd, along with their neighbours. A changed name keeps the old current in front: Marreth Desseki-Asken. Given names: **Sada, Corrin, Ubel, Marreth, Ilva, Dost**.

### How they regard each other

**The Kavren, the Weyn and the Yurrel** — *a three-cornered knot in which no two of them are ever the unit*

Weyn title, Kavren draw, Yurrel claim, one piece of ground. A Kavren household balancing westward buys a draw off Weyn land and treats it as an instrument — held eleven years, shed the moment the braid moves. The Weyn let it as a ninety-year obligation with the works attached and regard the Kavren use of it as a slow form of vandalism. Then the arm jumps and the Yurrel claim the walking right over that same ground, and the Weyn cannot answer because they do not hold the draw, and the Kavren cannot answer because they do not hold the title, and the Yurrel are already there and are correct. It has never once been settled by two of them talking.

**The Kavren and the Yurrel** — *improving, visibly, and everyone can date it*

Yurrel children born on the walking right in the east grew up inside the Suhr, and people raised on a ninety-second beat turn out to be very good at eleven-second work. Some stayed. Forty years ago a Yurrel who stayed was gone — struck from the shift, not mourned, not mentioned. Now they are counted as *walking wide*, a phrase about thirty years old that gets argued about at every gathering. They take Suhren and keep Ioman in front of it, and a two-current name is common enough in the east that nobody turns round any more. Among the Yurrel this is a live fight, and it is being lost, slowly, by the side that wants it stopped.

**The Kavren and the Weyn** — *cheerful mutual ridicule with no curiosity whatsoever in it*

They have dealt with each other every week for two hundred years. A Weyn thinks a people who cannot say where they live are not entirely serious. A Kavren thinks holding one patch of dirt for a thousand years while the sky changes over it is the funniest thing on the planet, says so, and gets laughed at in return. Neither has ever asked a question. Weyn children learn nothing about the Kavren. Kavren children learn the price of a draw and not one thing beyond it. They like each other. They have no opinions about each other. Outsiders keep waiting for one of those to turn out to be a lie.

**The Weyn and the Yurrel** — *getting worse, and both sides can show their working*

The arm's northern swing has come up short three cycles running: eleven hundred kilometres, then nine hundred, then seven hundred and forty. Shorter swings mean more claims, and the walking right has been invoked in the west twice in forty years where the Weyn arrangement assumes once in three generations. A draw-holder owes the works, the works cannot be kept up while somebody is living on top of them, and the second claim landed on families still behind from the first. The Yurrel did not shorten the arm and have nowhere else to be. The Weyn are not refusing and could not. Everybody on both sides knows the three numbers by heart.

### History

Six hundred years ago the crossings walked west. The tight braid, which had always been an eastern thing, slid into the dry country and put Suhr ground where the Weyn had had four hundred years of steady Dessek, and Kavren households followed it, because following it is what a Kavren household is for. The Weyn had a tenure form for ground and a tenure form for current and nothing at all for ground whose current changed twice in a working life. They made one badly, then again, then again. The draw as it now stands is the fourth attempt and dates from a hundred and ninety years ago.

Then the slack. For eleven years both strands thinned and the Suhr went quiet across the whole continent — no beat anywhere, and everything that needs the push simply not done. No Kavren household could be in set, because there was no third thing to be in set with. Households dissolved and were rebuilt afterwards out of different people, which is why so many eastern households are younger than their holdings, and why the Kavren are so relaxed about who belongs to what.

And the eleventh claim, sixty years back: forty thousand Yurrel onto ground held by eleven Weyn families, for nine years. It went better than anybody expected. Both peoples still reach for it when they want to be reasonable at each other, which is a sign of how the rest of it is going.

### The Null

Sindal is not touched. The lane serving it is one of the steadiest the ship knows. The honest problem is that Sindal has never been surveyed properly — instruments here need recalibrating hourly, a party costs four times what a world this size should, and the Census's chart of the braid is sixty-one years old and was accurate on the day it was drawn.

Four years ago attuned people in the eastern Suhr began saying there were three tones in the beat rather than two. The third is very faint. They disagree about nearly everything else and not about that, and — this is the part that bothers the Weyn measurers — they put it on the same bearing, and the bearing does not move with the braid. Set stone shows nothing. Census gear shows nothing. Two strands, exactly as charted.

The cost is specific and it lands on people. Suhr work runs on hearing the beat and hitting eleven seconds. Some of the best workers in the east now hear three tones and are out by a fraction, and a Suhr worker who has lost the window does not work. So the honest ones have stopped and the rest have not, and everybody in the east knows the count is wrong somewhere and does not know where.

Nobody is saying it is the Null. Somebody said it once and was asked not to again — not because it is forbidden, but because it is not evidence. Records has the trouble here it has always had with the deep-sighted: an entry resting on one person's ears is an entry the catalogue does not know how to hold, and three hundred years have not fixed that.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- Three beads on a cord at the belt, worked in the fingers all day and never looked at. Two together and one apart means the household is out of set. A Kavren will not raise it and will not stop touching it.
- A surname that does not match their brother's. Kavren siblings are named for wherever their mother was working that year, and they do not explain it, because to them there is nothing there to explain.
- They do not thank you. A Yurrel who accepts anything has claimed a right rather than received a kindness, and will spend the next week silently mending three things you had not mentioned were broken.
- A short iron rule, three fingers long, produced mid-conversation and laid against other people's stonework, joins and door frames. A Weyn will find your four millimetres.
- Counting under the breath. Anyone raised on the Suhr times work by the beat, and aboard a ship with no beat at all they still count, at ninety seconds, and cannot tell you they are doing it.
- Two currents in the name — Ioman-Suhren, Desseki-Asken. It means they changed, or that the sky changed over them and they stayed. Which of the two it was is the first thing another Sindal asks and the last thing anybody else knows to.

### Comedy

A Weyn crewmate re-set eleven courses of the third concourse stair overnight because they were four millimetres out, and it took two days to establish who. She apologised properly and then explained that four millimetres is four millimetres, and Structures explained that the stair is grown, and that the four millimetres is the tree. She has checked forty-odd joins since, has been right twice, and mentions the two.

And the counting. A Kavren in ninth-deck fabrication counts under her breath while she works, which the feline two bays over could hear perfectly well and took for a song he did not know. He started counting along. Four other people picked it up off him without ever hearing her. There is now a compartment where a dozen people pause at ninety seconds for no reason any of them can give, and the Kavren who started it came off that rota in the spring and it has not stopped.

## Ranohe II

*ordinary*

A flood-basalt world whose western continent descends to the sea in forty stone shelves, populated in its larger part by people who grow all their lives and therefore spend those lives going downhill. Inheritance, plumbing, marriage and money here are all answers to one question: how do you move somebody who cannot move themselves.

### Geography

One continent, Tesserane, and its western third is flood basalt: eight hundred stacked lava flows, weathered into a staircase. From the interior edge at 2,100 metres the land descends to the sea in forty shelves. A shelf is flat, three to nine kilometres deep, under metres of black soil. Between shelves stands a scarp — thirty to a hundred metres of columnar basalt, sheer, with a fall at every river. These are the Steps. Weather comes off the western ocean, so the Steps have rain most of the year and morning fog that burns off by ten. The fog belt sits on shelves nine to twenty-two and never reaches the top.

The rivers cut the scarps in gorges two hundred metres deep and forty across, wet, dark and permanently in spray. You cannot live on a shelf and also live in a gorge.

Behind the top shelf the staircase has one last riser: the Mirreth Wall, a single 220-metre flow, unbroken except where nine old channels cut through. Above it lies the Ombre — dry, because the rain is wrung out on the climb. Short grass, basalt pavement, wind from one quarter all year, hard frost from the ninth week.

Below shelf forty the land stops. Forty rivers arrive at once into the Halevet: drowned valleys, shifting channels, black sand, hot springs, forty kilometres of tidal mud. East of the Ombre lie nine hundred kilometres of salt and thorn, the Suvet. Nobody has needed to cross it.

### Peoples (4)

#### The Nevarren

**Cassine (semi-human)** · The Steps — the forty basalt shelves of western Tesserane

*Seed: The Alpine Ausgedinge, or Altenteil: the Austrian and Bavarian retirement contract, in which a holding is handed over during the holder's lifetime and the deed itemises exactly what the former holder is owed afterwards in named quantities — a room, so many litres of milk a week, firewood, the right to be carried. The borrowed element is the itemised, quantified, permanent maintenance written into the transfer itself; not who inherits, which on Ranohe nobody much cares about. Ranohe multiplies it. Here the transfer happens four or five times a life, the obligations never close, and they are cut into stone rather than written.*

A holding passes at threshold, not at stopping. When a Nevarren takes a new name they hand over the orchard, the shed and the shelf-right, and what goes with it is cut into the obligation-post at the head of the holding: nine measures of grain, water on the first and the fifteenth, somebody to come and turn them in the chair on Tendays. The duties are exact and permanent. A Nevarren of fifty carries six or seven of them, owed to four different givers. When somebody stops, the row is planed off the post, and an old holding's post is visibly slimmer than a new one.

It all runs downhill. You move one shelf at each threshold, into the house vacated by whoever went down ahead of you, so a Steps town is a queue and nobody has ever lived anywhere new. A Nevarren therefore has no shelf number, which everybody else on Ranohe has, and finds the idea of being from one place both restful and a little sad.

The descent itself they cannot do. That is not their work.

**Look.** Bark ridges dark and, on the wet shelves, lichened — moss takes in the deeper grooves of anyone past sixty and is tended rather than scrubbed off. Deep-set amber eyes. Everything they wear is built with folded seam allowance so it can be let out five times, which means an old coat is a small dark rectangle of original back panel between paler let-out strips, and anyone can read at a glance how long you have had it. Children are dressed the same way and are consequently mostly coat.

**Names.** Accretive. Two syllables at birth; each threshold adds an ending, chosen by whoever holds your obligations at the time from a local stock, so a full name lists the places your handovers happened. The endings -mni, -lu and -seth are upper Steps; -vas and -ani are Halevet; -kenn is given by a gorge fellowship and is always a story. Examples: Ota, Otarem, Otaremni, Otaremnivas; Resu, Resuvar, Resuvarinseth; Ova, Ovallen, Ovallenda; Anu, Anuveth, Anuvethani; Sehul, Sehulravelu; Tiro, Tiroskenmi.

#### The Kethuwe

**Amphibioid (semi-human)** · The gorges — the river slots cut through the scarps, chiefly the Ennesut, the Ravel and the Duvane

*Seed: The Balinese subak: an irrigation association whose membership is defined by drawing from the same weir rather than by living in the same village, whose flow is split by a proportional divider block with each member's entitlement cut into the stone as a count of notches, and whose labour obligation on the headworks is proportional to that count. Two elements are borrowed — proportional share as the unit of belonging, and a body whose jurisdiction follows the water and cross-cuts every other kind of membership. On Ranohe the water is not for crops. It is for moving people downhill.*

A Kethuwe holds notches. At the head of every stair stands a divider, a basalt block with the channel cut across it in teeth, and a family's share of the spring is a count of teeth. The count does two jobs: how much basin you may fill, and how many days you owe on the headworks. Nine notches, nine days.

The body that holds the stair is the fellowship, and a fellowship is not a village, not a gorge and not a family. It is everyone drawing off one spring. Two households on the same ledge can belong to different fellowships and attend different sittings, and they do.

Shares divide. That is how families grow, and it has a floor: under three notches you cannot fill a basin fast enough to keep a descent moving, so you pool, or you marry in, or you sell. A full descent takes three days, eleven basins and about forty people, and children learn the count before they read.

Nobody on Ranohe thinks gate work lesser work. What gate work is not is written on a post.

**Look.** Smooth-skinned, cool to the touch, webbed, with the amphibioid forearm iridescence gone faint from a life out of sunlight — gorge Kethuwe are pale in a way coastal ones find slightly alarming. Hair cropped to nothing, because gate work is done in a fall. One heavy belt carrying a tooth-gauge: a cut iron the width of the family's notch, for checking that a divider has not been quietly dressed wider. A Kethuwe who has sold their share keeps the gauge, and everybody notices, and nobody mentions it.

**Names.** A given name plus the number of the shelf you were born beside. Every Ranohe child born outside the Steps has one, and it is a cohort, not a rank — everyone who is Nineteen went to the same spring fair, and two Nineteens meeting off-world have something before they have anything else. Spoken short: Suri Nineteen. In full, at a sitting or at a bad moment: Suri, Ennesut, Nineteen. Numbers never change, which the Nevarren find restful and the Kethuwe find too obvious to discuss. Examples: Suri, Danev, Mehiu, Kaith, Oru, Peshet.

#### The Halevine

**Cassine (semi-human)** · The Halevet — the estuary at the foot of the Steps, its hot springs and black-sand shore

*Seed: The Tribunal de las Aguas of Valencia: irrigation water held as a turn rather than a volume, turns inherited and traded like property, and disputes settled in public in a single sitting by the holders themselves with nothing written down. The structural element is time as the unit of tenure. On the Halevet there is water everywhere and the scarce thing is a warm pool, so the turn is what got inherited instead of the water.*

There is no downhill on the estuary and no need for one. A Halevine elder spends the last thirty or forty years in a spring pool, where buoyancy takes the mass off and the hands keep working decades after a Nevarren's have stopped. The coast produces people still making instruments at two hundred and eleven.

What is held is pool-time, counted in longs — one pool from one high slack to the next, about six hours. Longs are inherited, traded, and argued out in public once a year in a single sitting, with nothing written down. A comfortable house holds nine a week.

An elder in a pool cannot fetch. Apprentices fetch. That is the whole bargain: you bring the clay, the meal and the wire, you are taught, the teaching is the payment, and everyone finds this obviously fair. A Halevine elder is surrounded by nineteen-year-olds. A Nevarren elder is surrounded by their own children, who are eighty.

The Steps call the estuary Forty-One. The Halevine have adopted it.

**Look.** The same bark ridges, but pale and smooth — the springs lay calcite down in the grooves, so a Halevine forearm is chalk-white in the lines and readable at fifty paces. Little clothing for most of the year: a wrapped waist-cloth, oiled shoulders. They stand in water so habitually that on dry ground they rock very slightly, which visitors read as impatience and which is not.

**Names.** Accretive like all Cassine, but every threshold crossed in a year is named on the same afternoon at the pool sitting, and everyone renamed that year is given the same ending. A suffix is therefore a date. Anyone sharing yours crossed with you and is your cohort for life, whether you can stand them or not. Examples: Rive, Riveshan, Riveshanole; Talu, Taluven, Taluvenari; Miha, Mihaset; Enno, Ennorav.

#### The Ombren

**Oni (semi-human)** · The Ombre — the high dry plateau above the Mirreth Wall

*Seed: The Sámi siida: a herding collective whose membership and pasture allocation are renegotiated each season, so the group you belong to is a decision taken periodically rather than a fact you were born into. The structural element is renegotiable belonging. The Wall concentrated it — with only nine ways down, the whole plateau does its renegotiating in the same fortnight, in the same nine places, at the edge of somebody else's country.*

There are no Cassine on the Ombre. Somebody who will one day be unable to move does not live where the ground freezes and the nearest help is forty kilometres downwind, so the plateau has never had elders in the Steps sense, and is the one part of Ranohe not organised around them.

A household camps with others by agreement, and the agreement runs one year. Every spring the plateau comes down to the nine breaches with wool, horn, tallow and cut post-stock, and in that same fortnight every camp group up there is taken apart and put back together by negotiation. Who you belong to is decided annually. Children are re-sorted with their parents and occasionally without, and a child who has wintered in four groups by twelve is unremarkable.

They read the ground by their horns. Flood basalt sits over a heavy current, and an Ombren routes a herd around what the pavement is doing the way anyone else reads weather.

**Look.** Deep saturated colouring — plum, rust, slate — heavy frames, weathered faces. Horns wrapped in felted cord from the first frost onward, because in certain autumn conditions standing bare-horned on the pavement genuinely hurts. The cord is whatever colour was to hand. It is not a code and outsiders keep deciding it must be. They lean: the wind on the Ombre comes from one quarter all year, and an Ombren standing indoors is still leaning about four degrees into a wind that is not there.

**Names.** A given name plus the camp group you first wintered with, which never changes although the group itself usually stops existing inside a decade. Half the plateau is surnamed for camps nobody can point to any more, and this troubles nobody. Below the Wall they are listed instead by the shelf at the foot of their breach, so an Ombren has a number in Nevarren ledgers that they have never used and would not recognise. Examples: Askol Tovren, Beshka Tovren, Umai Sellow, Hadrik Kavesse, Ritta Denn, Jonno Uskin.

### How they regard each other

**The Nevarren, the Kethuwe and the Halevine** — *three-cornered; no pair of them is the unit*

A descent needs all three, and the old quarrel only makes sense as a triangle. The post records that the heir brought their mother down, because a Nevarren obligation is to a person or it is nothing, and a fellowship is not a person. The Kethuwe are a count of teeth in a stone, which is not a person either, and that is precisely their objection. And every season a stair closes, more grandmothers go on the barge instead, which is money on the coast — so the Halevine have had an interest in the argument for four centuries and have never once spoken in it. When the Ennesut shut for a season four hundred and ten years ago, four elders stopped who would not have. All three tell it accurately. The Halevine tell it least.

**The Kethuwe and the Halevine** — *deteriorating, recently, with nobody breaking a rule*

Notches have always been transferable and it never mattered, because the only buyer was another family on the same spring. The port changed who has money. Coast families now buy notches in the lower gorges to secure a descent slot for their own elders twenty years ahead, and a fellowship that has sold a third of its teeth to people who will never work a headworks day cannot run a stair. Nine have folded on the Ravel since the channel was dredged. The Halevine paid a fair price at a public sitting for something openly for sale, and would like somebody to explain what they did wrong. Nobody can. It is still happening.

**The Nevarren and the Ombren** — *warm, and warming faster than either is comfortable with*

They meet for a fortnight a year at the nine breaches, and for four centuries it went well precisely because neither ever tried to persuade the other. Felt and tallow down; post-stock and two kinds of pear up. Then the port money reached shelf three. Fodder is cheap enough now to winter below the Wall, and the count of Ombren households doing it has gone from two to thirty-one in eleven years. Camps re-sort in spring, which means the re-sorting now happens with Nevarren families in the room, and about forty Nevarren households have agreed to a one-year membership in something — which by their own architecture is not a thing that can exist. Everyone is being very careful.

**The Kethuwe and the Ombren** — *no relation at all, and neither finds that strange*

There is no gradient above the Wall worth spending water on and nobody up there who needs moving, and there is nothing in a gorge an Ombren has ever wanted. So in nine hundred years the two have built no trade, no grievance, no marriages worth counting and no word for each other that is not borrowed from the Nevarren. Ask a Kethuwe what they make of the Ombren and you get the honest answer, which is that they have not thought about it, followed by mild interest at discovering this. The whole correspondence between them runs to about eleven letters a year, all concerning a spring at the Uskin breach that nobody disputes.

### History

For most of recorded history a shelf was a world. A Nevarren past their third threshold does not climb a scarp, so your last name was decided by whichever shelf you happened to be standing on when you got too large, and the shelves filled slowly with people who could not leave.

Nine hundred years ago four families on the Ennesut cut a chain of eleven basins into the gorge wall to bring one woman down two shelves to see a grandchild. She was Anuvethani and she is still named. It took nineteen days and it worked. They had pooled their water to do it, and the divider they cut to keep the pooling honest is the oldest one standing; the teeth are worn round and it is still in service. Within two centuries there was a stair on every gorge with water enough, and a Nevarren life stopped being a place and became a descent.

Everything follows from that. Holdings began passing downhill at threshold instead of sideways at stopping. The obligation-post took its present form. The barges started running the spring tides, and the coast filled with other people's grandmothers. And the Kethuwe, who had done it as a favour, found themselves holding the only work in the country that cannot be done without them, which is a difficult thing to hold well.

Four hundred and ten years ago the Ennesut closed for a season. Nothing was settled.

### The Null

Four lanes from anything that has gone quiet, and nobody expects that to change in a working life. What changed is everything upstream. Traffic that used to route through junctions which no longer exist now routes through the Halevet, and the Halevet took it: sixty hulls a year before, about nine hundred now, one yard become four, and the spring-tide barge become a dredged channel with a weekly boat. Estuary wages run roughly triple the Steps.

Nobody on Ranohe says out loud where the money comes from, and everybody has worked it out.

The sharper problem is domestic. A nineteen-year-old can take a boat from shelf three and be at the port in nine days, arriving at the bottom of a staircase their grandmother will spend a hundred and forty years descending, owing nine measures of grain a year to a post thirty-seven shelves above them. The posts say nothing about this. They were not built to.

Cassine serving off-world have all done the same arithmetic and none of them discuss it: the next threshold will happen somewhere other than home, and the name will be given by whoever is to hand.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- Measures doorways. Reads the clearance off any hatch or passage at a glance, remembers it for years, and will mention the tight one on a route you have both walked a hundred times. Does not know this is unusual.
- Gives things too large on purpose, and is quietly wounded when they are altered. A gift that fits now is, on the Steps, what you send to somebody who is not expected to need a bigger one.
- Will not shorten a name. Finds nicknames genuinely difficult, has to be argued into each one separately, and permits them as a favour rather than a habit.
- Has a plan. Knows within about two years when they will stop being able to do the work they currently hold, has arranged what happens then in boring and complete detail, and will not discuss it with you.
- Treats where you started and how old you are as public facts. Offers a number in introductions — Nineteen, and my mother was Twenty-Two — waits for yours, and is briefly lost when it lands as nothing. At home the age is written on the forearm in ridges anyone can count, so the first time somebody lies about theirs they are startled, and then startled again on learning it was not rude.
- Never lifts. Looks for the water, the slope or the counterweight before touching the object, on the unexamined conviction that anything heavy can be moved if you stop trying to pick it up. Also never stands with their back to a gate, valve or door that could close.

### Comedy

Off-worlders learn the naming convention within the hour and then overcorrect, using somebody's next name — which is not an insult but a promotion, and lands roughly like calling a nineteen-year-old "professor" in front of everyone they work with. It has happened at every first contact the Halevet port has ever hosted. The accepted recovery on the Steps is to take it very gravely and then ask the person to help carry something.

And there is the matter of Nevarren children, who are dressed in garments built to be let out five times and are therefore mostly coat. A six-year-old at the spring trading has the cuffs turned back to the elbow, stands on their own hem about once an hour, and goes over backwards, and every adult at the breach pretends not to have seen it, which is somehow worse.

## Casran Deep

*ocean*

An ocean nine kilometres deep with no land worth standing on, where the barrier that made the peoples is vertical: at eight hundred and eighty metres the water changes density, stops mixing and bends sound, so the two halves of the world have never properly heard each other. Everybody here is named for a depth, the four peoples have worked out four entirely different things to mean by that, and the oldest argument on the planet is six hundred years old and has never once been raised.

### Geography

No land. The charts say Casran III and nobody uses the numeral. The ocean runs nine kilometres deep and the only rock at the surface is the basalt spikes off the Ivresh Rise, white with lime.

Three barriers did the work.

The first is vertical. Between eight hundred and sixty and nine hundred metres the water changes density and stops mixing. This is the Sill. Above it: warm, lit, storm-driven. Below: cold, dense, black and enormously quiet. Sound bends at the boundary, so neither half can properly hear the other and never could. Under it runs a sound channel, axis at eleven hundred and fifty metres, in which a voice carries fourteen hundred kilometres.

The second is weather. The equatorial gyre is still — a floating weed mat two thousand nine hundred kilometres across and eleven metres thick, in permanent sun, ringed by a squall band a hundred and forty kilometres wide that has killed everything smaller than a town.

The third is ice. Both caps are sixty metres thick with six metres of freeboard; the northern advances two hundred metres a year to a calving edge.

Between mat and ice runs the Turn, a current ring thirty-one thousand kilometres round. A town in it makes nine kilometres a day and does not row. The Ivresh seamounts top out at eleven hundred metres, below the Sill, so everything that holds position here is shackled to something nobody has seen.

Each night the deep layer rises four hundred metres and sinks before dawn. Everything above the Sill eats out of that.

Depth is named, in eight bands — Ammis, Tolo, Verrin, Nurrey, Arren, Dolun, Kesht, Thessa — older than any of the four peoples, used by all of them, origin unknown, and the only thing the whole world agrees on.

### Peoples (4)

#### The Rhevani

**Amphibioid and sunfed in the main, with a long human minority. The mat is permanent sun and eleven metres of growing weed, so the sunfed take the far shifts on the outer columns, because less has to be carried out to them.** · The Mat — the still equatorial gyre, inside the squall band

*Seed: Ainu iwor: the river catchment held by a settlement as one indivisible unit from headwater to shore, where the right to it is conditional on obligation to the animals that move through it. Casran turned the catchment on its end. The animals move up and down rather than along, and the unit runs from the weed crown to the anchor foot nine kilometres below.*

Nothing on the Mat is owned by a person. A stage — four to sixty households on one platform — holds a column: the weed at the crown, the whole depth of water beneath it, and the anchor foot on the rock. It is one thing. You cannot hold the crown without the foot, and there is no word for trying.

What the column owes is the rise. Every night the deep layer comes up four hundred metres and sinks again before dawn, and between dusk and dawn nothing crosses the middle of a column — lines slack, nets up, everything taken at the edges. All food on the Mat comes out of that. A stage that takes from the middle is not fined. It is a stage nobody will cable beside.

Anyone crossing a column asks leave. It is always given and it is always asked, and eleven thousand times a year somebody's boat stops for four seconds in open water to say so.

**Look.** Sun-bleached to the point of looking underexposed, whatever the underlying colouring, and salt-stiff cloth gone pale at every seam. Everyone wears a line-harness, indoors and out. Everyone has the same two calluses in the same two places from the same shackle key, which is how one Rhevani identifies another across a room. They stand with one hand on something.

**Names.** A given name and the band you work. A person holds nothing on the Mat, so the name is the whole of what is yours: Sevas Tolo, Anhe Verrin, Toril Ammis, Vessa Nurrey. Moving band is the event of a Rhevani life — Anhe carries Verrin-Nurrey for a year or ten, and then one morning writes Nurrey-Verrin and waits to see who follows her. Doing it early is the great embarrassment. Doing it late is a sadder thing people notice and do not mention. Merek Arren-Tolo went the other way at fifty-one, out of the Sill work and up, and is still asked about it.

#### The Okolu

**Human, oni and amphibioid in roughly equal parts, and the only bird folk on the world, who nest on the masts and on the basalt spikes and are the reason anyone knows what the spikes look like from above.** · The Turn — the mid-latitude current ring

*Seed: The kula ring of the Massim: the inherited exchange partnership, a lifelong opposite number on a fixed circuit, where the relationship outranks whatever moves across it. Casran made the circuit physical. Nobody can overtake, so the partner you inherit is one you cannot swap, avoid or reach early.*

Eleven towns, one circuit, nine years. You meet the same towns in the same order and cannot change the order, because the Turn will not let you pass and rowing against it buys you nine kilometres a day of nothing.

So everything is credit. A debt contracted at one meeting comes due at the next, nine years off, and your opposite number is inherited rather than chosen — your mother's partner's son is yours, and if you cannot stand him that is a fact about your life and not a problem with a solution. The relationship outranks anything moving across it.

The great offence is being out of position. A town that falls behind is waited for; the fleet will go slow for a year to let a bad hull rejoin, will never mention it afterwards, and everybody will remember which town it was for four generations.

They do not accumulate. There is nowhere to put it.

**Look.** Layered and patched, because cloth arrives only when the town that makes it does. An Okolu coat carries four towns' worth of repair and reads, to another Okolu, as an itinerary and a rough date. They are visibly wrong-footed on anything that does not move — they call it the sticking — and stand as though waiting for the floor to start.

**Names.** A given name and the band of the seabed under the town at the hour you were born, which is the same thing as saying how far round the circuit that was. Rayun Thessa was born over the trench; Nissa Dolun over the Rise. Sabek Kesht, Omur Thessa, Delhe Verrin, Farrey Arren. Verrin is the shallowest sounding anywhere on the circuit, a sixty-kilometre shelf, and being born over it is a small lifelong distinction nobody can justify. Arren is a forty-metre window and there are nine living. You cannot change band — you were born where you were born — unless you leave the Turn, and then you take the band of wherever you stopped, and it goes second, always, for the rest of your life.

#### The Senthe

**Amphibioid, pressure-adapted over a very long time, and rock skins, who need nothing done to them at all. A few Made, built for it, with the worst parts problem on the planet. The Senthe amphibioids and the Rhevani amphibioids are the same body and share nothing else whatever, and have to be introduced.** · Below the Sill — the flanks of the Ivresh Rise, and down into the Karesh Trench

*Seed: Mande jeli lineages: the hereditary professional line that holds spoken knowledge and is bound by standing obligation to particular houses it speaks for. Casran added a constraint the jeliw never had — the channel carries one voice at a time across a whole basin, so the line that holds the knowledge also holds a slot in a queue.*

Below the Sill a voice put into the channel properly carries fourteen hundred kilometres. One at a time. Five lineages hold the training that gets a voice in, and each speaks for particular settlements — routes, pressure tables, genealogies, who owes what — cannot refuse them, and is fed by them for life. Neither end can end it.

The queue is the institution. Who speaks, in what order, for how long, is settled a season ahead and is the one thing the Senthe are inflexible about. Talking over somebody is not rudeness. It is taking a thing that cannot be given back.

A Senthe leaves four seconds before answering anything, because that is how long an echo takes to clear a basin. They do it in a corridor, in a lift, in bed. Outsiders read it as disapproval for about a year and then stop noticing.

**Look.** Undecorated and pale from a lifetime with no light, squinting at everything. Almost all of them keep the resonator collar at the throat, which does nothing whatever in air. They speak low, slowly, and leave the gap. Rock skin Senthe run to the dark greys of the Rise with one thin white seam where the brine layer sits.

**Names.** Every voice is trained at the channel axis, so a Senthe's first band is always Dolun and the second is where they live and speak for: Rukh Dolun-Kesht, Anje Dolun-Thessa, Metta Dolun-Kesht, Solvei Dolun-Arren, and Damen Dolun-Dolun, which is not a joke and he is tired of it. Reversed — Kesht-Dolun — they are speaking for the settlement rather than for themselves, which is an entirely different thing to say. Everywhere else on Casran reversing your bands announces you have changed over, so the rest of the world reads a Senthe at work as somebody making a late and dramatic life decision, and the Senthe have given up correcting it. The lineage — Vashni, Kolun, Oberai, Sereth, Tamm — is stated separately, and is the part that actually matters.

#### The Korveth

**Human and amphibioid, a large Cassine population, and the rock skins who cut the halls and can stand against fresh ice for an hour taking measurements.** · The northern cap — halls cut into moving ice

*Seed: The Carolinian navigator schools, Weriyeng and Fanur: two complete rival systems taught as whole bodies of knowledge, entered by graded initiation, where a master's line holds a claim on you for life and a grade is paid in labour rather than money. Casran changed the subject. What is taught is not where you are. It is when you end.*

A hall is cut nine kilometres in and reaches the calving edge in about forty-five years. Everyone knows their hall's year. It is not morbid; it is the number you plan around, and children can recite the whole cap's worth before they swim well.

Reading the ice is a trade with two schools, Havek and Ushen, and they do not agree. Havek cores the ice and reads the flaw structure: a date with a range, accurate far in. Ushen surveys the hall's position against the Rise every spring and extrapolates: a date that moves each year, accurate near the edge. Both are right where they are right and both deny it.

You are initiated into one school by grades, you do not change schools, and a grade is bought with nothing but cutting — a season on your master's hall, with your master cutting beside you. A hall commissions a reading, gets two, and picks one to plan on. That choice is remembered longer than the people who made it.

**Look.** Wind-burnt across the face and nowhere else. Everything a Korveth owns has a handle or a becket on it, including the cups. They will hand you a thing you admired with no ceremony at all and be baffled by the fuss. They carry less than anyone, lose less than anyone, and are very hard to impress with a house.

**Names.** Fifty-four of the cap's sixty metres are under water, and the Korveth cut those fifty-four into the same eight names the rest of the world spends nine kilometres on, because what a band tells you is how far you are from the top of your own world. A hall at forty-six metres is Thessa. Ede Thessa-Dolun, Askar Verrin, Moru Dolun-Kesht, Vieni Kesht, Hallas Tolo-Verrin. When a hall calves you cut a new one and take a new band, so most Korveth wear two or three in a life and an old one has four. When Ommeth went, forty people reversed their names in a week and thought nothing of it — which is the whole difference between the cap and everywhere else, because on the Mat that is the event of a life and here it is what happens when the ice runs out. A Cassine Korveth runs two naming systems at once, ends up with something enormous, and enjoys it thoroughly.

### How they regard each other

**The Rhevani and the Senthe** — *a courtesy neither side has ever confirmed, and it is coming apart politely*

Eleven thousand tensioned cables in a current strum, and the note goes down, not up. Nobody above the Sill has ever heard it. Below, the Senthe have lived six hundred years under a singing ceiling, and the channel they speak across runs along it.

Twice a year the Rhevani slack every cable to re-seat the shackles. For eleven hours the basin is quiet, and every lineage's long business goes into it. The Senthe have sent formal thanks for three centuries. The Rhevani accept thanks they do not understand and have decided that asking would be rude.

Forty-one stages have now moved to staggering the slacking by section. It is better for the cables. The quiet is down to four hours and shortening, the thanks are getting warmer, and both peoples are being extremely courteous about it on the way down.

**The Rhevani, the Okolu and the Korveth** — *a three-way account, and no two of them are the unit*

Four centuries ago Hanneth was being waited for — bad hull, fleet going slow, everybody kind about it. A Rhevani stage cut one of its own anchor cables and gave it over so Hanneth could hold position and refit. Under Rhevani law a column cannot be parted. Marreth has run three degrees off true ever since and has never repaired it, because the account is open and everybody on the Mat knows which column that is.

Hanneth went north. Their descendants are the Korveth. The Okolu never refilled the slot and still stop four days in open water where a town would have been, for the plain reason that recutting the calendar costs more.

Each version is true and each flatters the teller. And once a generation a Rhevani raises the outstanding cable with an Okolu, warmly, and cannot understand what just went wrong. A Rhevani and a Korveth have never met and could not find each other's region on a chart.

**The Okolu and the Senthe** — *warm, and it took about a minute*

They meet over the Rise at four of the eleven rendezvous: metal and vent salt up, cloth and dried mat down, all of it on nine-year credit, which suits both.

What they actually recognise in each other is the shape of the thing. An Okolu inherits an opposite number and cannot swap him. A Senthe inherits settlements and cannot refuse them. Neither institution can be left, both were scheduled before either of them was born, and both peoples find this so obviously the correct way to live that the conversation ends almost at once with everyone pleased.

Ask an Okolu about the cables and they will say the Rhevani should simply be told, and be genuinely surprised that this is not a contribution.

**The Senthe and the Korveth** — *getting better, recently, by accident*

Under the cap there is no Sill — cold from the ice down, one column, the only place on the world where the two halves touch. The Senthe come up and the Korveth cut down, at nine holes re-cut every spring. They have been in each other's houses for six hundred years and had never once asked a question. Food and stone, and nothing else.

Then somebody noticed that a Senthe voice reflects off the underside of the cap and that the reflection reads flaws sixty kilometres out. Havek and Ushen are both competing for Senthe partners now, which is quietly changing what a school is. The Senthe find it astonishing that anyone wanted the echo, which they had always regarded as the annoying part.

**The Rhevani and the Okolu** — *warm, with mutual condescension, three weeks at a time*

Once a circuit the Turn brings the fleet past the mat's edge, and for about three weeks boats cross the squall band in the one window it allows. Nearly everything that happens between these two peoples happens in those three weeks, including most of the marriages, which is why Okolu children arrive in cohorts nine years apart and why there is a whole body of song about it, all of it bad.

The Mat thinks the Turn are people who could not hold a position. The Turn thinks the Mat are people who stopped. Both are said fondly and both are said constantly. Also: a Rhevani asks leave to cross a column and an Okolu cannot be stopped from doing anything at all, and the three weeks are full of small collisions neither side takes seriously.

### History

Six hundred and forty years ago a vent field opened on the Ivresh Rise and pushed cold water up through the Sill for thirty-one years. The surface got nutrient it had never had. The mat thickened, the Rhevani roughly doubled, and the cable count went from about two thousand to eleven thousand, where it has stayed. Below, the basin filled with noise and has not been quiet since. Both peoples can name the year. To the Rhevani it is the good one, and children learn it before they learn the bands.

Four hundred and twelve years ago the town of Hanneth cut out of the Turn on a borrowed Rhevani cable and went north for stone. Their descendants are the Korveth. The Okolu never refilled the slot, and the fleet still stops where Hanneth would have been. It is not a memorial, nobody pretends it is, and it has been cheaper than the alternative since before anyone alive.

A hundred and ninety years ago the northern cap sped up — a hundred and twenty metres a year to two hundred, over two decades — and every reading on the ice was wrong at once. Nine halls came up short. Nobody was lost, because the Korveth flit fast, but nine halls' worth of people reached the edge with nothing cut and had to be taken in, and the cap has not forgotten who took whom. The argument about why it sped up produced Havek and Ushen, who have not agreed since.

Twice in living memory a Korveth has gone down to stand at the stop. Both were fed until they could not move, and neither has ever explained what they thought they were doing there.

### The Null

Threatened, in the way that means nothing has happened. The temperature is the same. The mat is the same thickness. The slacking happened on schedule and the ice is moving at the rate it has moved since anyone alive started measuring.

Two things are slightly wrong, and they are the same thing. The channel is carrying further than it should — voices that used to stop at fourteen hundred kilometres are arriving at nineteen. And the reflection off the underside of the cap is coming back clean where it used to come back smeared. Two lineages think it means something and three think it is a cold year. The Ushen masters have started giving halls longer dates and are pleased about it.

Nobody has put the two observations together, because the Senthe do not write things down and no school tells the other school anything.

Off-world, the lane serving Casran has thinned at the far end. Two of the eleven *ARC 017 - Star Singer* has lost were upstream of here, and the song past the system is fainter than when the survey came through.

She came once, spent four months over the Rise, added nine hundred and forty entries and signed eleven Casrani aboard. She is not expected back.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- The two calluses — base of the right thumb, outside edge of the left forefinger, from a shackle key. Another Rhevani spots them across a room and says so immediately, as an opener, before a name.
- The four-second gap before answering. It is echo discipline, not disapproval, and on a ship where everyone fills a silence a Senthe gets talked over about forty times a day and has stopped expecting otherwise. *ARC 017 - Star Singer*'s watercourse also runs at very nearly the Mat's note, so a Senthe aboard either cannot sleep at all or sleeps better than they ever have, and finds out which in the first week.
- Asking leave to cross someone's work — a bench, a bay, a line of sight through a scope. A Rhevani does it on day one, gets a strange look, does it again on day two, and it takes about four years before the compartment starts asking back without noticing it has started.
- Nine-year credit. An Okolu writes down a favour and means to settle it in nine years and will, exactly, to the item, and the other party will have forgotten entirely by then and will find the whole thing frightening.
- Everything they own has a handle. A Korveth will give you a thing you admired, on the spot, with no ceremony, be confused by the thanks, and has no idea this is unusual anywhere.
- The ship has no depth. A Casrani aboard has a first band and nothing to put after it, so they have started using ring numbers, and the argument over whether the hub is the surface or the bottom has both a real case each and has been running eleven years.

### Comedy

The last-year party for Ommeth Hall has been held four times. Havek said thirty-one years, Ushen said twenty, the hall planned on Ushen and threw the party, and then went on existing. The second one was defiant. The third was a joke and a good one. The fourth had a noticeably smaller turnout, and the host counted, and everyone could see her counting, and nobody could think of a single thing to say.

A Rhevani away from the Mat clips on to furniture. Not deliberately — the hand does it while the rest of them is talking. There is one at a refectory table attached to a chair leg right now, who will discover it standing up, who will be mortified, and who will do it again next week.

## Tessine IX

*ordinary*

Tessine IX turns once in sixty-three hours under two point seven bar, and what comes out of that is a wind running three hundred kilometres an hour in one direction that has never been recorded going the other. Five peoples live along it, and what divides them is a nine-hundred-kilometre range and whether you have ever stood behind it, because everywhere else the air has never once been still.

### Geography

Sixty-three hours to turn, two point seven bar at the coast, two thirds of a standard gravity. A slow world under thick air makes one circulation, and it has never been logged going the other way.

**The Gathe** runs three hundred and four kilometres an hour above the boundary layer, fifteen hundred and forty kilometres from a cold basin to a rain coast, and has never been measured below two hundred and sixty. It does not weather the land. It planes it. Everything vertical on Tessine is what the Gathe has not removed yet: **skells**, rock ridges thirty to a hundred and seventy metres high all pointing the same way, and, where it has cut through a spur, towers standing alone with two hundred metres of lee behind each one. The lees are where people are.

**The Chelve** stands athwart it — nine hundred kilometres of range, crest two thousand four hundred metres. The windward face is bare rock and the lee face is worse, because the Gathe comes down it. But behind the centre, where the air goes round instead of over, are **the Stillings**: five valleys, forty to ninety kilometres long, floored in loess, where the air moves at walking pace and a flame stands up.

Upwind, the **Peshoun** basin is going down two and a half millimetres a year. Downwind, the **Laum** coast is building seaward seventeen metres a year out of it.

Anybody who has not been to the Stillings has never felt still air.

### Peoples (5)

#### The Cirene

**Mixed, and the mix is the point. At two point seven bar a built wing beats a born one over fifteen hundred kilometres, so an angel on the Lomara flies the same rig as the humans, felines and oni beside her and uses her own wings for handling and the landing. Tesk fliers take the light urgent loads and are the best on the planet by wing loading. Aurel Cosma's wings buy him nothing at home that a good frame does not.** · The Lomara: the first eighty kilometres of skells at the head of the belt, where the noses stand highest and the air comes in clean.

*Seed: Venetian and Genoese pilotage: passage through a named water is compulsorily in the hands of a closed body licensed for that water and no other, granted on demonstrated knowledge rather than seniority, fixed in number, and the pilot personally answerable for what happens on it. Tessine made the water one-way. A line cannot be practised — you fly it or you do not, and you cannot go back up it — so what a licence certifies is knowledge of an hour rather than of a place, and it is worth nothing forty kilometres either side.*

A cargo wing is nine metres across, weighs less than the person under it, and is left at the coast, because nobody carries one home. Seven hours down. A hundred and thirty-one days back round the end of the Chelve, three times a year, until you are too old for the road.

Direction is free. The whole of the trade is *when*, and a when is a line: a named entry at a named height at an hour read off the dust coming over the third tower. There are nineteen lines out of the Lomara. Each carries a fixed number of licences, and you fly the line you are cut for and no other, however many years you have flown the one beside it. Most Cirene hold one line for life. Two is a career.

A licence is not seniority and cannot be waited out. You are cut one when three holders say you can read that line's hour, and any of the three can be publicly wrong about it afterwards, which is the reason there are three.

**Look.** Everything cut to go under a harness: no collar, nothing across the chest, nothing in a breast pocket. Permanent pressure callus at the collarbone and the hip crest, which is the tell that survives any amount of time away. Pale cloth, because the belt is bright and the floor throws it back. Squint lines that go right round. And the long flat stride of somebody who has walked the flank road three times a year since they were seventeen.

**Names.** A house name that never changes and a given name that changes with the licence. The stocks are known and ungendered, so a name tells you the grade before it tells you anything else: Ilinca and Tavi are children's names, Miren and Zenaida belong to hands on the ground, Aurel, Corin, Petru and Vessa are cut with a line. Aurel Cosma was Miren Cosma until he was twenty-two. Surrender the licence and you take back the name you had before it, which nobody reads as a demotion and everybody reads as the end of a career. People who knew you on the ground use the ground name for life, and mean by it that they knew you on the ground.

#### The Yorsk

**Rock skins are heavily represented and it buys an hour in the open rather than a house — everybody lives in the lee regardless. Humans, oni and amphibioids in the same lee towns doing the same work, and the rock skins are simply who gets sent out when something has to be done in the sand. Everyone loses the top of their hearing by about forty and nobody has ever thought of that as a difference between peoples, because until recently it was not one.** · The deep belt: fourteen hundred kilometres of skells and towers between the Lomara and the fans, lee by lee.

*Seed: The waqf: property taken permanently out of ownership, its yield dedicated to a named purpose, administered by a post rather than inherited, and the founder's written conditions binding on everyone who holds that post afterwards. On Tessine what gets dedicated is a lee. The belt has no soil, no water and nothing anybody would call an estate — the only wealth in it is two hundred metres of standing room and the machine on the nose above — and because a lee cannot be owned it cannot be lost in a bad year, which is the whole reason there are people along fourteen hundred kilometres of it.*

A skell nose at full height stands in three hundred kilometres an hour. A Yorsk machine takes about a fifth of a megawatt out of that and has to do it without ever stopping, because nothing in the Gathe can be stopped and started again. There are four thousand one hundred of them in the belt and not one is owned by anybody.

Each was dedicated, mostly a long time ago, by somebody who wrote down what the take was for and who was to keep it. The conditions are binding and they are specific. The lamp at Herrek is to be lit by a person who has never flown; fewer Yorsk have never flown now, so it is lit by an eleven-year-old, and everyone is satisfied that this is correct.

A keeper holds the post, not the lee. You cannot sell it, borrow against it, leave it to a child or take it with you, and you can be put out of it by the same three sentences that put you in.

**Look.** Buff-grey by the end of any day spent out of the lee, and it does not entirely wash. Goggles pushed up on the head indoors, which nobody has ever stopped doing. Hair cropped or covered. A rock skin who has worked the belt carries one thick belt-coloured layer laid over whatever their forearms used to say, which is the ordinary price of the work and not a thing anybody complains about.

**Names.** Names accumulate rather than replace. You are given one as a child, one when you are trusted alone on a nose, and one when you are put into a post, and you keep all of them, oldest last: Dannow Pruskel is Dannow now and was Pruskel at fourteen, which is the age he was when the Herrek nose went. Which name a person uses on you is the whole of what they are telling you. Strangers get the current one. Somebody about to ask you for something uses the oldest. Hessek, Tobbrin, Weddon, Gellet, Arrat, Nusk.

#### The Zaumet

**Amphibioid, human, canid and feline in roughly even parts. The webbing pays for about one month in nine, when a channel switches and somebody has to be in it; the rest of the year it is fourteen metres of rain on everybody equally.** · The Laum: the fans and braided channels where the Gathe runs out over the sea, and the coast that is still arriving.

*Seed: The German Walz, the journeyman's compulsory tramping years: you leave at a set age, you may not go home, you own almost nothing, you work where you are taken on, and the circuit is finished before you are. Tessine gave the circuit a direction and then changed what it measures. The walk goes upwind because the Laum is made of the Peshoun, but the thing a Zaumet is actually reaching for is not distance. It is stillness, which lies off the road, behind a range, and which most of them never get to.*

A Zaumet house stands on skids and twenty people move it in a morning. Channels switch without much warning, a bank that carried a street last year is open water now, and the coast is a kilometre and a half further out than when the oldest people alive were born.

At about sixteen a Zaumet leaves, walks the flank road upwind, and does not come home for six years. You work where you are taken on. You turn where you turn.

The far end of it is the Stillings, two days off the road through the Chelve gap, which nine living Zaumet have reached. Almost everybody turns a long way short, and nobody counts that against anybody, and the sentence most quoted in the Laum is a Bessaun one — *turn here* — said kindly, at Kerreth, to people who were going to find out anyway.

What you have to have done is stand in the Peshoun and watch the ground come off it, because you will spend your life building on that ground and it has not finished arriving.

**Look.** Oiled cloth and nothing that cannot be wrung out. Bare feet indoors, because the floors move and you want to feel it. Forearms cut about to a permanent texture from withy and split cane, which is everybody's second trade. Everything they own goes in one bag that hangs rather than sits, a habit from six years of putting it down on ground that might be wet.

**Names.** A given name from the stock of your grade — leaver, turned, taken on, and the one you get when you first house somebody else's years — and a road name, which is where you turned and never changes. Twenty-two living people carry Chaveu, the last water on the flank road. Nine carry Ondevé. Betaume Chaveu, Elue Poteau, Soravin Duvay, Thenau Ondevé, Jussel Poteau. People who walked with you use the given name you had on the road for the rest of your life, so you can tell across a room exactly which six years two Zaumet have in common. And plain Ciraun, who came home in her second year to a sick mother and has never been asked about it twice.

#### The Bessaun

**Mixed, with no pattern the Census has found beyond the cold — the Peshoun wants layers rather than a body plan. Deep-sighted people are over-represented and have been for a very long time, which is a professional fact about reading a face and nothing else.** · The Peshoun: the cold high basin upwind of everything, going down two and a half millimetres a year and uncovering rock nobody has seen.

*Seed: Maritime salvage: a person who saves property from peril is never paid a wage and never agrees a price. The award is set afterwards by people who were not there, scaled to the danger accepted and the value actually preserved, and it is nil if nothing came out — no cure, no pay. Tessine supplies the peril on a schedule. A face comes clear over a season or two, is workable for one or two more, and is then gone, so everything anybody has ever taken out of that basin was taken against the wind's clock, and none of it was ever priced beforehand by anyone.*

A face uncovers, and it is not yours, and you may not leave it. What you choose is which one, and that choice is the whole of a working life.

Nobody is hired and nobody quotes. A gang works a face out and then three people who were nowhere near it set the share: what came out whole, against what it cost to get it, against what the wind was doing that season. A gang that works six months and brings out a ruined face is paid nothing at all, and is not thought less of for it, and is not paid.

So every Bessaun has a figure for everything, arrives at it afterwards, and gives it unprompted. Deep-sighted Bessaun read mineralisation nobody else can see and are awarded on it like anyone. That one person's eyes can decide a share is not a difficulty in the Peshoun. It is a difficulty every time a Bessaun tries to explain it anywhere else.

**Look.** Wind-split lips that never quite heal, and layers, and more layers. Lamp soot worked permanently into the creases of the hands and the lines round the eyes, from a working life spent close to a face with a lamp. The deep-sighted push their shades up onto their foreheads indoors and forget they are there.

**Names.** Given names come from a stock that turns over every eight years, so everyone called Turrim is within eight years of everyone else called Turrim and age is audible. You are renamed out of the current stock the first time an award goes against you, and every time after that. An eight-year-old and a man of fifty-eight can both be called Ospen, and the room knows immediately which of them it is and what happened. Hetten, Draske, Obbin, Turrim, Neshka, Ospen. Never being renamed means you are young or you are careful, and careful is not a compliment. Friends use the old one, which means: I remember when you were right.

#### The Aunek

**Mixed and unremarked; nobody there has ever been asked to account for it and the question lands oddly. Two bodies are professionally visible. Felines can place a sound in three dimensions, which is worth nothing anywhere else on the planet and a great deal in a valley where a bearing can be heard going. And the Stillings are the only ground on Tessine where bird folk fly, which every bird folk on Tessine knows.** · The Stillings: five valleys in the wind shadow behind the Chelve, forty to ninety kilometres long, floored in three to eight metres of loess.

*Seed: The Japanese iemoto: a craft held as a named school whose head licenses everybody permitted to practise it, grants each of them a name built out of their own, takes a share of what every licensed hand earns, and is succeeded by someone who takes the head's name entire, so the name outlives its holders and is the school's principal asset. The Stillings turned this into a question of surfaces. Still air is the only place on the planet you can lap a true flat, grind a lens or set a bearing to anything, so the technique that keeps the rest of Tessine running cannot be taught outside five valleys, because outside them the air moves.*

The loess runs three to eight metres deep and none of it started here. It needs no irrigation. The Aunek are almost certainly the largest population on the planet and the Census has spent five months with them against nineteen years on the axis, which is a fact about the Census.

There are nine schools. Every flat, bearing and lens on Tessine came out of one of them, and none of them can be moved, because the work is the air. The oldest is Vimai, which has had a head of that name for three hundred and ten years and is on its ninth.

They can hear. Everyone else on the planet is missing the top of their range by forty and the Aunek are not, and they did not know this about themselves until somebody came and measured it. They found it mildly interesting. What they noticed first, and much earlier, is that visitors talk as though the room is against them.

**Look.** Unweathered — no squint, no split lip, no dust in the creases — which reads to everybody else as improbably young and is simply what a face looks like when nothing has been blown at it. Fine cloth, uncovered heads indoors, and a lamp carried openly rather than boxed. They keep their hands out of pockets and off surfaces, from a lifetime of not putting a thumb on anything that has been lapped.

**Names.** A given name, and a school name granted by the head, built from a syllable of the head's own and regranted at each of the four grades — so an Aunek's second name changes three times and always toward the same person. Odrune Tai, Helmo Nendai, Imoune Chimo, Wessune Perrai, Pallo Vimai. The head's name passes entire and unaltered to the successor, which is why Vimai is three hundred and ten years old and the woman holding it is thirty-four. Below the first grade you have no school name at all, and people who taught you then go on using no school name at you for ever, which is either affectionate or extremely pointed and is never anything in between.

### How they regard each other

**The Bessaun, the Cirene and the Zaumet** — *a three-way with no pairs in it, and shifting*

What a Cirene reads on the launch ground is dust, and the dust is Bessaun. Which faces were opened in the Peshoun this season decides what the Gathe carries; what it carries decides how a line's hour reads; and what does not fall out on the way lands in the Laum and becomes Zaumet ground.

Thirty years ago the wind uncovered a long run at Kerreth and the Bessaun have been working more face than at any time on record. Lines are harder to call than they were. The fans at Tourlé are building faster than the figures the Zaumet inherited.

None of the three has an arrangement with the others. There has never been anything to have one about.

**The Yorsk and the Cirene** — *one-sided, and neither side minds*

The Yorsk think about the Cirene constantly. Nineteen lines pass over the belt, a wing overhead is the clock, and a Yorsk in a lee names the hour from which line just went by and is out by four minutes. A wing in trouble sheds its load, it comes down in the belt, and the Yorsk keep it. The shelter at Pirrec is roofed in eighty-seven panels of assorted ages, and the keeper can date most to the decade.

The Cirene do not think about the Yorsk. They cannot land, have never landed, and there is no version of the trade where they could. Nobody has taken offence. A Yorsk asked whether they might would want to know what for.

**The Yorsk and the Aunek** — *actively improving, and not free*

Since the Gathe went through the Chelve saddle, the belt to the second Stilling is two days instead of eleven. The machines have always run on Aunek bearings. What changed is how many, how fast, and how often anybody goes in person: a keeper now meets the person who ground her flat, which was unheard of in her mother's time.

It has gone extremely well. Both peoples are slightly embarrassed about how well.

The cost is that the second valley is windier than it was. Not much. Measurably, and measurably more each decade. The Aunek in it know they are being paid in traffic for a portion of their own stillness, and they have not decided, and nobody has stopped.

**The Cirene and the Aunek** — *mutual blank*

A Cirene on the Tourlé line crosses the third Stilling at nine hundred metres, twice a day, and has done for four hundred years. No Cirene has met an Aunek. No Aunek has met a Cirene.

There is no reason for it. The lines do not land there, nothing the Aunek make goes out that way, and the flank road runs the other side of the range. Asked about the Aunek, a Cirene describes the shape of the valleys from above, accurately, and stops, because that is the whole of it. Asked about the Cirene, an Aunek says they go over about noon.

They are the only two peoples on Tessine who have nothing whatever to say about each other.

**The Bessaun and the Aunek** — *warm, and nobody calls it anything*

Every few years a Bessaun comes into the Stillings to have something ground true and stands in still air for the first time, usually somewhere in their forties, and it goes badly. Not frightening exactly. A lifetime of the basin does not prepare anybody for hearing their own clothes.

The Aunek have handled it the same way for a very long time: you are walked outside, kept talking, and nobody mentions it then or afterwards. There is no word for it in either language and no obligation either way.

Ask an Aunek why and they will explain that the valley sounds different in the first hour, which is not the reason, and which everybody involved finds entirely sufficient.

**The Yorsk and the Zaumet** — *structural, and older than either side's opinion of it*

A Zaumet on their years walks fourteen hundred kilometres of belt and sleeps most of it in Yorsk lees, and is fed in a good many, because the dedications say so. The one at Weddon's nose specifies anyone on foot going upwind between the ninth hour and the thirty-first. It was written four hundred years ago by somebody whose reasons are not recorded, it is binding, and the keeper has no power to close it and no wish to.

The Yorsk do not regard this as hospitality. It is a condition. What they do have views about is walkers who restack the fuel wrong, which is all of them, for about the first year, and which is corrected by pointing.

### History

The wind is older than any record and has not changed inside one. The ground has.

There is a mark cut into a face at Ipren, and the Bessaun have recut it and written down the figure every generation for two hundred and twenty-six years. The ground beside it is down fifty-seven centimetres: two and a half millimetres a year, which was nobody's guess before and is everybody's arithmetic now. Everything the Laum is made of came off the basin at that rate.

The flank road was assembled over four centuries and its history is huts. It was nearer three hundred days once. Every reduction since is somebody's shelter on somebody's stretch, and the oldest, at Poteau, has been rebuilt on the same footing more times than the Zaumet have counted.

Tourlé has been moved four times and now stands nine kilometres from where it started, all of it seaward. Nobody calls those different towns.

Ninety-one years ago the Gathe finished cutting the saddle at Chelve and went through. It is the only thing in living memory the wind has completed rather than continued, and everyone alive can tell you what their grandparents made of it.

The Census came five generations back, and what it left that mattered was an accurate clock. Hours had been called against a sun that is up for thirty-one hours at a stretch. The calling changed inside a decade, the Lomara has argued ear against instrument ever since, and both sides are still flying.

### The Null

Nobody has noticed, and on Tessine that is not carelessness. It is the one place where the Null's signature is indistinguishable from the weather.

The Gathe takes things and leaves nothing behind. A nose fails and there is no wreckage — the lee and everything in it are downwind and gone inside the hour, and the Yorsk word for the site afterwards is the same word they use for clean rock. A people whose ordinary sky erases without residue has no shape in its language for erasure without residue.

The Lomara cut four off-world line-licences over the last sixty years, on the reasoning that a lane is a line. Two of them go to places that are not there. Both licences are live, because a line ends by surrender or by re-survey and you cannot re-survey a line you cannot reach, so the holders remain the holders and the count for those lines remains one.

Vessa Bilea holds the second. She is sixty-one. She has not surrendered it, nobody has suggested she should, and asked what the line is like she describes it accurately and in the present tense.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- The name they left with is the name they have. A rank name is cut at home and only at home, so a Tessine aboard keeps whatever they were on the day they got on the ship, indefinitely. Aurel Cosma has been Aurel for nineteen years. He was Miren until he was twenty-two, and there is nobody within three galaxies entitled to call him it.
- Too loud, and standing wrong. Everybody off the Stillings grew up in a permanent roar and it costs them the top of their hearing by forty. A Tessine crewmate stands with one shoulder forward, watches mouths, answers half a beat late, and says *I heard you* considerably louder than they mean to.
- Points instead of speaking. A shout in the belt goes downwind and is gone, so a Yorsk points, and goes on pointing, and says nothing. Anyone who works with one learns inside a week to look where they are looking before asking what.
- Answers *where* with a time. Ask a Tessine where anything is and you get an hour and a direction — twenty past, from the aft stair — and never a distance, because at home direction was free and the only thing worth knowing was when to go. It follows that a wrong *when* is the only real error there is, which is why Aurel Cosma will concede anything except a timing call, and why he cannot see that the crew think that is the small part.
- Prices your work afterwards, sincerely, with a number. A Bessaun who has watched you do something difficult will go away, weigh what came out of it against what it took out of you, and tell you — days later, warmly, as the largest thing available to say. It is meant as respect and it has never once been received as respect.
- Leaves a compartment better than they found it, in specific ways nobody asked for. Everyone who has walked the flank road keeps hut etiquette for life: refill what you did not empty, restack what you did not use, trim the lamp. A Tessine crewmate will reorganise Ilka Voressen's bench exactly once.

### Comedy

Lines get their names from whoever made them memorable, and memorable is nearly always bad. The second-fastest line out of the Lomara is named for a man who read the hour an hour early, missed the fans entirely, came down in the sea and was picked up by people cutting bait. He lived. He was delighted. His grandchildren introduce themselves with it. Nobody involved regards this as a consolation prize, which is why Aurel Cosma takes *going Cosma* as a promotion and why nobody aboard has worked out that at home it would be one.

The other one is the air. A Tessine spends their first months aboard checking whether it is moving — the corner of a napkin, a sleeve, the edge of a chart — all day, without the faintest idea they are doing it. Somebody eventually asks. The asking is the bad part, because after that they cannot stop watching themselves do it, and it gets considerably worse for about a fortnight, and everyone is very kind about it, which does not help at all.

## Ombrea V

*ordinary*

Ombrea has no hills, so its water is slow and its walls are made of salt and of timing: rain that falls inland in the second month reaches the coast in the seventh, and which water rules you decides your whole calendar. Everything that falls into warm fresh water here is gone inside a season, which is why the only two places on the world that keep anything are the acid end of the gradient and the salt end, and why both of them ended up as archives.

### Geography

Warm, wet and flat. The highest ground on the inhabited plain is the surface of the **Pelho**, eleven metres; the world's highest point is a shell ridge on the **Ghal** at thirty-eight.

The **Adu** falls thirty-one metres in its last twelve hundred kilometres, so its flood travels about nine kilometres a day. Ombrea has two waters: the near water, which is rain where you stand, and the far water, the same rain months later. Every people is governed by one of them.

The Adu opens into the **Fan**: two hundred and seventy kilometres of tidal channel and bar, remade yearly. Upstream the **Duvak** takes the far water and is grass for eight months and an inland sea for four. West of the Fan the **Sevu** is the reverse — salt creeps a hundred and ninety kilometres inland for eight months, and the far water drives it out for a hundred and eighteen days.

North, above the salt entirely, the Pelho is a rain-fed acid dome, pH 3.9, where nothing rots. West along a cold current the Ghal is seven hundred and sixty kilometres of fog and evaporite pan, rained on twice a decade.

Salt is the wall. A changed-over amphibioid needs nine days of graded exposure to cross from fresh into salt, longer coming back, which on a world where amphibioids do the moving makes nine kilometres a three-week journey. Thin sea ice at both poles, and two peoples at least on the second continent; the Census has met one.

### Peoples (5)

#### The Dareme

**Amphibioid majority; humans and rock skins throughout, and nobody counts** · The Fan — two hundred and seventy kilometres of tidal delta that is a different shape every year

*Seed: West African rotating work-exchange (esusu, aro) — a fixed set of households who work each other's ground in a fixed order of turns, where the order is the asset and membership is the only security anybody has. On the Fan the order cannot be fixed to land, because the land moves, so it is reset every year by where the silt is going.*

A turn is about two kilometres of channel and its bars, and everybody on the rotation works everybody's turn in sequence. Let a channel silt and go still and it goes anoxic, and anoxic water goes downstream into somebody else's. So the order matters, and it is not inherited. It is reset each spring by whoever is currently best at reading where the silt is going, everyone knows who that is, and it is nearly always somebody under thirty-five.

The tool is a **kewe**: a metre of flat notched iron, for cutting bar and opening side channel. Planted upright in the bank it means something else. It ends whatever the planter is obliged to — a turn, a debt, a marriage, a post — at once and completely, and nobody may stop them, and nobody may ask why. The one thing it does not end is the name.

The Fan swings near-fresh to near-salt twice a day. Dareme amphibioids grew up crossing that line and think nothing of it, which is why the Dareme do most of the world's carrying.

**Look.** Amphibioid Dareme are smooth-skinned and cool to the touch, and Fan water is heavy with iron and tannin, so the webbing at the base of the fingers stains a permanent rust-brown that no scrubbing shifts. Rock skin Dareme carry the Fan at the bottom of the log — ochre banded over black, the first band and usually the widest — with everything since laid over it. Everyone wears the same short wrapped work-tunic, quick-drying, and nobody has ever built a Dareme garment with a hem.

**Names.** A personal name and the name of whoever vouched you onto a rotation: said in that order, said first, carried for life. Plant the kewe and you may walk away from a turn, a debt, a house or a marriage and nobody may ask why — and your voucher's name goes with you, so the Fan can always see whose judgement it was. A voucher may withdraw, at the crossing, in front of people. One-name Dareme exist, every Dareme in the room notices inside a sentence, and most are vouched again within a few years by somebody else, which is also visible. Abike Sunmonu, Fayimi Adawe, Morenke Okande, Bankole Iremide, Adunni Bewaji, Toyese Sunmonu.

#### The Iskavi

**Human majority, a long-settled oni population, and about a third amphibioid** · The Duvak — six hundred kilometres of flood basin on the Adu, grass for eight months and inland sea for four

*Seed: Amazigh collective oath (tagallit) — a charge is answered not with evidence but with a stated number of people willing to swear alongside the accused, the number rising with the seriousness, and everything turning on how many will actually stand there. On the Duvak the canvass is impossible for a third of the year.*

For eight months the Duvak is grass and the Iskavi are impossible. Grazing boundaries are argued at length. Wells are disputed. A feud will run three generations on a misplaced fence and everyone involved can recite the fence.

A charge is answered by oath-numbers. You state how many will swear alongside you — not that they saw anything, nobody needs to have seen anything, they are swearing to you — and if you raise the count you are clear. Seven for a fence. Fifty-three for a death. Every Iskavi keeps a private running estimate of their own number and adjusts it after every argument they have.

Then the far water comes, and for four months the only ground is about three hundred known mounds, and nobody can canvass anybody. You go into the water with a charge against you and you come out of it knowing.

The amphibioid third do the moving in flood: messages, medicine, salvage, a body. The one thing they will not carry is an oath-canvass. That was a fight once and they won it.

**Look.** Heavy-boned and weather-cured, humans and oni alike, everybody in the same felted flood-coat, which goes into the water as often as not. Forearms carry fine white scarring from cutting drain through root mat. Iskavi go barefoot from the first rise of the water to the last and are quietly superior about the state of other people's feet.

**Names.** An Iskavi is called by their own name and the name of whoever stood first for them the first time they were charged with anything. It is a name you acquire by being accused, so an adult with one name has never been. They are called clean, and it is not a compliment: it means nobody has ever had to find out. Flood-born children are all named in the fortnight the water drops, which puts a batch of them into the world together every year. Alvezh, Dumek, Dushen; Karsu Ozhan, Pirune Dumek, Vesht Alvezh, Dushen Ozhan.

#### The Sevuk

**Amphibioid and human in roughly equal number, and a Made population that arrived with the first lift pumps three centuries ago and has been Sevuk about as long as anyone** · The Sevu — a hundred and ninety kilometres of coast that is salt pan for eight months and fresh for a hundred and eighteen days

*Seed: The sea loan (foenus nauticum) — capital advanced for one named venture at a heavy rate, where the loss falls on the lender and the debt dies with the voyage. On the Sevu the venture is a season and the sea is the sea.*

A season is financed, not owned. The digging, the seed, the pumps and the hired hands are put up by somebody who is not working the ground, at terms fixed before the water turns, and if the pulse comes weak and the salt does not go out the season is closed. The loss sits with whoever put up the money. Nothing is carried into next year, nobody is ruined and nobody is owed. Everyone backs and everyone borrows, usually in the same year, and a Sevuk asked about something from six years ago will have to genuinely think.

The one obligation the Sevu cannot close is the Made, whose parts do not care what season it is. There is a fund for that. It is the only standing thing on the coast and the Sevuk are shifty about admitting it stands.

Amphibioid Sevuk are in the water a hundred and eighteen days a year and dry for the rest. They mind. The Sevu has more shade and more standing tanks than the rest of Ombrea put together.

**Look.** Everyone on the Sevu is marked at the wrists and ankles, where eight months of salt crust meet four of standing in fresh and the skin never quite settles either way. Amphibioid Sevuk go pale and slack-webbed by the eighth dry month and are visibly better company from the hundred and eighteenth day onward. Clothes are light, loose and replaced yearly because salt eats them, so nobody on the Sevu owns anything old — which visitors read as poverty and which is arithmetic. The Made are heavily lacquered against the salt and get relacquered at the turn of every season, in company, badly.

**Names.** A personal name and the name of whoever stood the loss on your first season — the backer, not a parent, and very often somebody who lost money on you. It is said first and it is said cheerfully, so a Sevuk introducing themselves is telling you who was wrong about them and how long ago. Losing it happens when a backer's terms are voided, which is rare and ugly: a Sevuk with one name is not shamed, but cannot be backed, which is the same thing in practice, and the coast usually arranges something within the season. Nerau Asenu, Simat Waruh, Dolek Kilem, Ravai Oyet, Meku Asenu, Tuvai Sendah.

#### The Pelhoi

**Human majority, an oni minority settled long enough that nobody dates it, and rock skins who came for the cold and stayed** · The Pelho — a rain-fed acid dome eleven metres above the plain, five metres sixty deep, cold, and nothing rots in it

*Seed: Isnad — the chain of transmission, where a statement's authority rests on the unbroken list of who had it from whom, and criticism is of the chain rather than the content. The Pelho is a chain you can dig, and the Pelhoi have turned the method on the ground.*

A raised bog lays down a millimetre a year, so depth is date, and everything that ever fell in is still there. Pelhoi cutting fuel come up with harness, a tally, a boat, a hand, a stand of trees five metres down still standing, leaves on.

A face is worked by two people from different households. One cuts, one reads, and they may never be the same person, because a chain with one person in it is not a chain. The reader logs depth, order, and who handled what. A find with a broken chain is not a poor find. It is nothing: the object is perfectly fine, there is no use for it, and it goes back in. Nobody regulates cutting speed, because you cannot cut without a reader. What the Pelhoi argue about is whether a fast reader is a good one.

The Pelho has no season. Rain is the only water and it falls all year, so the Pelhoi are never busy at any particular time — most of why everyone else's records ended up on the bog.

**Look.** Pale from nine months of cloud and heavily clothed in oiled wool that smells of the bog and is not apologised for. The hands are the tell: bog-black to the wrist, and the stain is thought well of. Rock skin Pelhoi came for the cold, because a bog face can be sat against for a whole shift, and the Pelho's grey-brown sits narrow in the log because most of them arrived as adults. Almost no amphibioids: nothing changes over in water at pH 3.9, so the Pelho is the one part of Ombrea that is reached on foot.

**Names.** The given name is read off something in the peat — cut into a haft, a tally, a strake — and is in use once at a time across the whole bog, going back on the list when its holder dies. There is one Aune on the Pelho and everybody knows which. The second name is your first reader, because the first link in your own chain is whoever logged you. A reader later found to have logged badly takes down everyone they read: the second name goes, and a one-name Pelhoi is not shunned, they are simply not usable as a link, which is worse. Off-world Pelhoi meet a second Aune and are quietly disoriented for about a week. Lemmi Saarka, Aune Vimmel, Urmas Routsi, Hessel Ruuk, Reeta Saarka, Tarvo Vimmel.

#### The Ghalen

**Rock skin majority, humans throughout, and a Tesk population going back as far as anyone's records** · The Ghal — seven hundred and sixty kilometres of fog coast in the lee of a cold current, salt and evaporite over the fossil beds of a delta that died thirty-eight million years ago

*Seed: Ottoman gedik — a fixed and limited number of licensed working positions, themselves heritable and sellable property, without which you do not practise. On the Ghal the position is not a shop. It is a face in a bed, and it has an end date.*

The beds are old delta: thirty-eight million years of Adu, dried, pressed and gone to something between coal and stone. They are cut at a face, and the face is the property.

There are seven hundred and ninety-four faces and there have been for three centuries, the number fixed when the beds were opening faster than the water could support. It rains on the Ghal twice a decade. Fresh water comes by barge from the Sevu inside a hundred and eighteen days and there is no second chance in the year. A face can be inherited, sold, lent for a season, left to a nephew. You cannot open a new one. Plenty of Ghalen work faces they do not hold and nobody thinks anything of it, because holding is about who answers for the water.

A face works out in about a working life. Everybody here has inherited a countdown and has planned around the year it ends since they were eight. Tesk hold many faces; a seam a hand's width thick is a face nobody else could have opened.

**Look.** Rock skin Ghalen have the Ghal at the bottom of the forearm — pale evaporite grey seamed with the near-white of old salt, the first band and usually the widest — with everything since laid over it. A Ghalen who left at seventeen and worked three worlds is genuinely difficult to place, which two of them will establish across a room, slowly, and be delighted about. Everybody covers against the dust, faces wrapped from the first hour of the working day, and the tan line at the eyes never entirely goes.

**Names.** A Ghalen picks their own given name at about fourteen and keeps it. The second name is whoever first put them on a face, and since there are seven hundred and ninety-four faces the whole Ghal runs on about forty second names, so anybody can be placed in one breath. Ghalen are therefore the only Ombreans with a ready answer when somebody asks what they would like to be called, and they find everyone else's difficulty with the question strange. They are also the only people here who can change half a name without touching the other half; some do, at forty, and it is discussed for years. Hesar Oskeli, Nella Igrell, Tamin Zerune, Revi Oskeli, Solek Dhoven, Feri Tovask.

### How they regard each other

**Dareme ↔ Iskavi** — *getting worse every decade, and both peoples can see it happening*

The Iskavi have cut drain for eight hundred and forty-seven years, and more each decade, because the flood is what took their people and every cut is made by somebody who buried somebody. The far water now reaches the Fan faster, thinner and shorter, and no longer turns the outer channels. A turn that cannot be worked comes off the rotation, the household plants the kewe, and nobody may ask why. Seven outer islands have emptied in four generations. No villain here, and neither side pretends there is. What they still do together is argue the date: their two days for the same pulse have drifted six apart, and they have it out in person, at volume, at the crossing.

**Iskavi ↔ Sevuk** — *nine kilometres apart and they have never met*

The same pulse drowns the Duvak and freshens the Sevu. The Iskavi work the eight months the Sevuk cannot and the Sevuk work the hundred and eighteen days the Iskavi are afloat, and between them is a levee and a salinity step that costs an amphibioid nine days each way. Nothing is wrong. They think well of each other on no evidence at all, exchange nothing, and both know perfectly well that a light-pulse year is a good decade on the Duvak and a ruinous one on the Sevu. Marriages happen about twice a generation and one of the pair has to stop working. Asked whether this is sad, both peoples look blank and then slightly impatient.

**Dareme / Pelhoi / Ghalen** — *three-way; no pair of them is the unit*

The middle of the world keeps nothing — a trunk down on the Fan is unrecognisable in eleven weeks — so what Ombrea knows of itself sits at the two ends of the gradient, with peoples holding half each. The Pelho holds the oldest writing, and nobody can say a word of that hand aloud, because nothing in a bog records sound. The language is Dareme, descended, still spoken by people who keep nothing at all. Over the Ghal's faces lie four metres of modern pan laying a countable couplet a year, and it disagrees with the Pelho by ninety years in four thousand. Any result needs all three, no pair can produce one, and they have worked by post two centuries.

**Sevuk ↔ Ghalen** — *improving fast, and the Ghalen are still deciding how they feel about it*

The Ghal's fresh water comes by barge inside the Sevu's hundred and eighteen days and there is no second chance in the year. Nine years ago the pulse came late, the barges did not come at all, and a backer named Kilem voided the season's terms — ordinary on the Sevu, astonishing on the Ghal, where nobody has written anything off in three centuries. The Ghal argued about it for two years and then started taking Sevuk terms on water. Sixty-one faces are on them now, up from four. What the Ghalen have not settled is whether a thing that can be voided is a thing you can plan a life around, and they discuss it constantly and with visible enjoyment.

**Iskavi ↔ Pelhoi** — *an open argument about method, roughly eleven hundred years old*

An Iskavi charge is answered with a living count: who will stand with you now. The Pelho holds chains of the dead, and a Pelhoi chain can establish a fact about a Duvak household four hundred years back with a precision no oath-count can touch. About once a decade somebody under charge produces one. The Iskavi have never decided whether that is evidence or cheating, and both positions are serious — an oath-count measures who will stand there, which is the entire point, and a chain measures what happened, which is a different thing and not obviously less useful. The Pelhoi, asked, supply the chain and decline to say what it means.

### History

Ombrea burned once. Two metres eighty down in the Pelho, right across the bog, there is a band of charcoal eighteen millimetres thick, and above it two hundred and thirty years with no tree pollen in it at all. That is two thousand eight hundred years ago, on a world where standing timber does not catch. Nobody has an explanation. Every southern people has a flood story from that period. None mentions fire.

Eight hundred and forty-seven years ago the far water came into the Duvak and did not leave for twenty-two months. It took most of the herds and an uncounted number of people, and when it went the Iskavi began cutting drain and never stopped.

Two hundred and six years ago the pulse failed twice running and the salt did not leave the Sevu for two years. Every season on the coast was voided, which is what the arrangement is for, and it held, which is the only reason there is still anybody there. The standing terms date from the year after.

Three centuries ago the Ghal was being opened faster than the water could carry, and the faces were fixed at seven hundred and ninety-four.

Seventy-eight years ago the Census arrived and Ombrean rot ecology went from a local competence to an export. The closed loops on half the survey fleet were designed by people who learned them on the Adu. Ombrea's principal export is graduates, and the Fan's outer islands empty faster for it.

### The Null

Ombrea is fine. The damage is nineteen years upstream and it arrives here as arithmetic.

The Chaverre lane closed nineteen years ago, three systems up. Ombrea was never on it; Ombrea was on the branch it fed. The run that used to come in on a two-hundred-and-fourteen-day rhythm now comes the long way round on three hundred and ten, and three hundred and ten does not sit inside the Ombrean year the way two hundred and fourteen did. Arrivals precess. Loading on this world happens in the Sevu's hundred and eighteen days or it does not happen, so a ship now lands inside the window about two years in three, and which years is not knowable more than one ahead.

That lands hardest on the Ghal. A Ghalen has known since they were eight roughly which year their face runs out and has planned every year since around that one, and now that year may or may not have a ship in it. Some of them have gone onto Sevuk terms for exactly this reason. Nobody calls any of it a Null effect, because nobody here has seen one.

The other thing is small. A bog is never empty — there is always pollen, always dust, always insect casing. A layer with nothing in it has only ever been read as a fault in the cutting, so the note goes in, the face is abandoned, and nobody goes back, because a broken chain is nothing to go back for. There are five such notes on record and the oldest is from before anybody now alive. Several readers have started keeping their own list of them, separately, without mentioning it to each other. At least two of those lists have six entries.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- Says who vouched them before anything else — not the discipline, not the post, the name of the person who stood for them, in the first sentence — and then waits for yours. An Ombrean takes *nobody, really* as a genuinely sad answer, and is too polite to pursue it, and you can watch them not pursuing it.
- Reads water by eye and is faster than any instrument aboard. Colour, what is growing on the surface, what is not, the way it turns off an edge — a Dareme will give you a pool's stage and roughly how long it has left, and be right, and be unable to show any working whatever. They will take the instrument's number if you insist on it. They will not change their answer.
- Cannot leave anything still. Turns settling jars. Tips buckets. Stirs a pot they are not cooking. Standing water on the Fan goes anoxic and anoxic water goes downstream into somebody else's, and the hands do it well before the head is involved.
- Closes a year and means it. A Sevuk will not accept an apology for anything more than a season old — they look at you with real confusion and tell you it is shut — and cannot be made to carry a grudge, which is restful for about a month and then quietly maddening, because neither can they be made to carry a favour.
- Says *twenty-six years* at a broken plank before they can stop themselves, meaning how long the material took to lay down. And refuses: an object with no provenance is not a poor record to a Pelhoi, it is no record at all, and they will say so once, flatly, and not move, and they are immovable about it in a way that has cost two of them postings.
- Looks at a catalogue entry and asks, sincerely, what the specimen has turned into since. An Ombrean assumes that anything left alone becomes something else and that this is the correct outcome, so a record that holds a thing still is a record of something that has stopped.

### Comedy

A Dareme postgraduate spent a fortnight on the Ghal quietly turning every settling jar in an assay shed, because she cannot leave standing water alone and did not notice she was doing it. She had been introduced as very senior. The Ghalen assumed it was a technique, adopted it, and were still doing it two years later when somebody finally asked her what it was for.

Somebody on *ARC 017 - Star Singer* stood a spare shovel upright in the aft grove to mark where the seedlings were. Three Ombreans saw it before anyone thought to explain. One of them wrote home about it.

And a second-year from the Ghal, asked by a lecturer to introduce herself, gave her name, her voucher, her voucher's voucher and the year her face runs out, because she assumed the question was the one it would have been at home. The lecturer said thank you. Other people have introduced her that way ever since, and she has decided to find it funny.

## Belune III

*ordinary*

A shelf sea shaped like a funnel, twenty-six metres of tide twice a day, and seventy-three kilometres of seabed that exists for five hours at a time and will pass a walker but not a cart. Five peoples sorted by that window — four living inside it, and one who went west to the only ground on the planet with no tide at all and became, by accident, the people the galaxy thinks are Belune III.

### Geography

One moon, Yerel. Ordinary tide. Everything after that is the shape of the basin.

Two-thirds of the northern hemisphere is shelf sea nowhere deeper than fifty-two metres, and the Bight of Yarkan is its funnel: seven hundred and twenty kilometres at the mouth, closing to a throat you could shout across. The head comes up twenty-six metres forty, and twice a day seventy-three kilometres of seabed is walkable for about five hours. Its channels move every year.

The barrier is that window. It passes a walker and refuses a cart, a herd, a child under about nine, or anybody carrying more than eleven kilos at pace. So the flats move people and news, and every load goes the long way round.

East, the same swing meets the Radna Wall: two hundred and forty kilometres of cliff, no shelf, and a tide that arrives rather than drains — a front two metres seventy proud at thirty-four kilometres an hour.

South, the Tuura is the flattest great river measured anywhere. Its bore runs a hundred and ninety kilometres to Aitsu; below Aitsu the river reverses twice a day, above it never.

Further south the Uvane Chain is a drowned range: one landmass at low water, eighty-six islands at high, sixteen knots between.

West, past a hundred and sixty kilometres of waterless salt, the Qalin is the floor of a sea that left — the only ground here that does the same thing every day. Ice at both poles, peoples on both.

### Peoples (5)

#### The Beluni

**Beluni (semi-human), many of them tidal — shifting between feminine and masculine with their mana turning when they do; also humans, and a long-settled feline minority who work the fog watch** · The Yarkan flats — seventy-three kilometres of seabed at the head of the Bight

*Seed: Igbo otu ogbo age-grades: everyone born inside a span of years is one body for life, carrying public duty and discipline sideways between equals rather than downward from elders. The terrain chose the span and made it load-bearing. The channels across the flats migrate, so a route is perishable knowledge and a seventy-year-old's expertise is a way to die — seniority therefore cannot be authority, and the cohort became the only thing on the planet that knows where the flats currently are.*

The channels move. A route that was safe at fifteen kills at fifty, and the mud that took your grandmother is now the way across.

So the unit is the cohort: everyone born inside the same three years, one body from birth to burial. It re-walks the flats every spring and the result lives in about thirty heads. The cohort ahead teaches the sky, the sound and the arithmetic, and refuses to teach the route, because theirs is wrong now.

A crossing is walked as a line, sixty metres between people, so that fog cannot take two of you at once.

You repair what you walk past. Nothing is assigned and nothing is counted, so the traps within an hour of shore are immaculate and the far ones are held together with whatever was to hand. Everybody knows. There is no mechanism.

Debts are counted in minutes, said aloud, never written. A cohort below fifteen cannot hold a line. It folds, and its members join a younger one as the slowest people in it, or they go.

**Look.** Olive to bronze, and salt dries white on the forearms by the end of a working turn; some wash it off and some cannot be bothered. Heavy-lidded dark eyes built for flat grey light — a Beluni reads shape in fog where nobody else reads anything, and squints miserably under ship lighting. Hands cold to the touch, always, and they do not know that is unusual. Nothing loose below the knee, and nothing carried that could not be dropped. A knotted cord at the left wrist, pulled through one knot per interval. Nothing goes on the floor, ever, including aboard a ship that has never had a tide in its life. The attuned place each other across two hundred and fifty metres of fog and the felines place everything else. Tidal Beluni look identical either way — same height, same face, same voice — and only the mana turns.

**Names.** One given name from the family, one cohort name shared by everybody born in the same three years. The cohort names run a fixed list of forty-seven that comes round every hundred and forty-one years, so a second name gives a Beluni's age to within three and there are always two Aydars alive a century apart. Sevim Aydar, Melis Aydar, Doruk Kerimli, Naz Toskan, Efe Ustek, Baran Bayindir. The first two are eleven months apart, are not related, and will both tell you so before you ask.

#### The Zhurat

**Bird folk on the upper ledges; rock skins on the low ones, which flood** · The Radna Wall — two hundred and forty kilometres of cliff coast east of the Bight, worked from ledges

*Seed: Cornish tin tributing and the setting: every pitch offered at a fixed interval, small self-formed partnerships bidding down the share of the take they will work it for, and the partnership carrying the entire risk of a pitch that turns out barren. A vertical coast supplied the interval and removed the survey. You cannot walk a ledge before you take it — you read it from the opposite headland in bad weather — so the whole of the skill is judging a thing at four hundred metres and then being unable to take the number back.*

A rope is seven to twelve people who have agreed to be seven to twelve people. Ledges are set once a year, at the first low after the year's highest tide: a ledge is named aloud, ropes say what share of its take they will work it for, and the lowest said takes it. Said cannot be unsaid.

Nobody has been on it. You read a ledge from the headland opposite and a rope that reads well eats.

A barren ledge is worked anyway, all year, because a ledge left alone weeds over and is barren next year for somebody else.

Inside a rope the split is equal and stays equal for anyone hurt on it, which is why choosing who is on your rope takes longer than choosing a ledge.

The bore comes west along the rock and you count it in. A lookout thirty-eight kilometres up the Wall buys sixty-seven minutes. The interval is taught in songs, the songs are not revised, and every child is told separately, out loud, by an adult, to take twenty-two minutes off.

**Look.** Mostly bird folk: barred grey over slate, white at the throat, small and light even for bird folk. Nothing loose is worn — close harness, short sleeves, bare feet with soles like boot leather — because cloth that catches wind on a ledge is how people go off it. They trim their own flight feathers and let nobody near them with a blade, which began as a plain rule about who is on your rope and has long outlived the reason, so a Zhurat's primaries are always slightly uneven, because nobody can do their own well. The lowest ledges flood and are worked by rock skins, who take a soaking and a knock that would finish a hollow-boned person. A Wall rock skin's arm opens with a broad near-black band of the Radna's own mineral and everything above it is wherever they went afterwards, which is why one who left at seventeen and worked four worlds is nearly impossible to place.

**Names.** Given name and a patronymic in -ovna or -ovich, taken from whoever taught you the ledges rather than whoever bore you, which is usually but not always the same person. Reva Sokolovna, Milen Yavorovich, Zlata Bratinovna, Bogdan Ilchevich, Nika Dremovna, Ostap Venkovich.

#### The Lohva

**Amphibioid mostly, with humans and a good many Beluni bodies** · The lower Tuura — a hundred and ninety kilometres of river that runs both ways

*Seed: Scandinavian skjutsplikt, the turn-based duty to convey: the obligation to carry a traveller or a load to the next stage falls by a public rotation, cannot be refused, ends cleanly at the handover, and earns nobody any thanks. The Tuura took the geography out of it. On a river that reverses, the next stage is not a place but whoever is going the right way in the eighteen minutes the water is still, so the turn falls by direction rather than by address, and nobody on the reach is ever more than half a day from owing one.*

For about eighteen minutes at each turn the lower Tuura stops moving, and everybody on a hundred and ninety kilometres of river is working at once — moorings, transfers, handovers. Then it runs the other way for six hours and you cannot follow anyone who has left.

So nothing travels with its owner. The turn is called at the slack by whoever took the last one, out loud, and the person named takes what is on the bank whether it suits them or not. Refusing is not a thing there are words for. Choosing is.

Your part ends when the next pair of hands closes. What you failed to carry you say at the following slack, standing up, to whoever was waiting, and that is the whole of the sanction and it is enough.

Every boat has a shelf under the thwart that is never the boat's own. Running empty is not wrong. It is peculiar, and people ask.

**Look.** Amphibioid mostly, with humans and a good many Beluni, and asking which is which will get you an answer about a boat instead. Dark from the water and never entirely dry. What you notice is the bearing: a Lohva watches the river over your shoulder throughout a conversation, and at the turn they stop talking and go, and come back. Rope callus on both hands in the same two places, from hauling in one direction and then the other. A short overshirt that comes off in one pull, because a person who goes in on the run has about a second to decide what they are wearing.

**Names.** An adult is called by their eldest child. Aruve becomes Taavetuva — parent of Taavet — and answers to nothing else in company. Adults without children take the same form from whoever raised them, in -ken. Aruve Taavetuva, Meelik Sanderuva, Virve Kaisauva, Lembit Harmiuva, Nuuri Sanderken, Taavet Nuuriuva. Your own given name survives only in the mouths of people who knew you before, which is a small and shrinking number, and hearing it is not sentimental. It is information about who is talking.

#### The Deshaal

**Sunfed, humans and rock skins** · The Qalin — a salt basin below sea level, west of the coast across a hundred and sixty kilometres of waterless ground

*Seed: Somali xeer and the diya-paying group: a body collectively and without limit liable for a member's debts and injuries by customary agreement rather than by any state, entered and left by consent rather than by blood. The waterless crossing moved what the group visibly does — its working function is carrying water out to meet its own people — and the unlimited liability surfaced in speech, as the rule that a person may not state their own name.*

A Deshaal does not say their own name. Somebody else says it, and by saying it they have stated they would cover what that person costs, which is not a figure of speech — the group is liable together and without limit for a member's debts and damage.

Membership is not blood. You can be taken into one at fifty, by agreement, usually because you are in trouble, and released from one, which is grave, formal, and done in daylight with everybody there.

What a group does that anybody can watch is water. It sends carriers out into the salt to meet its own people coming in, at a day and a distance agreed beforehand, and if you are not at the place they keep going, because standing still out there with what they are carrying kills two people instead of one. Everybody knows this before their first crossing and nobody softens it.

The pans are worked by the group and the salt is the group's. What one person owns is a coat and a set of tools.

**Look.** Sunfed, humans and rock skins, and nobody in the Qalin thinks the distinction worth a remark. The sunfed do not cross the salt — a dormancy landing halfway is a body — so the sunfed of the Qalin have never in their lives been anywhere else, and are the people most offworlders meet. The basin's rock skins come up salt-pale and faintly pink from the pans, which is why the galaxy believes Belune III rock skins look like that; the Radna ones are almost black and nobody offworld has ever seen one. Everyone wears one long unbelted coat woven by the group, and the weave reads at twenty-five paces to a Deshaal and to nobody else alive. Headcloth against the glare, kept on indoors.

**Names.** One name, and you do not use it. Someone else says it for you, and the saying is the commitment. A Deshaal arriving somewhere with nobody to name them will simply wait, and the customary answer is that a stranger does it, and the stranger has then done something real. Wessa, Toran, Amel, Idrit, Kenu, Sorel. Offworld they acquire a second name almost at once, from whoever wrote it down first, and they keep it, and it means nothing whatever to them.

#### The Uvane

**Humans, amphibioids and a good many Beluni bodies** · The Uvane Chain — eighty-six drowned peaks in the far south

*Seed: The Benedictine horarium: a day cut into fixed offices by a signal outside anybody's control, where inclination is irrelevant and the whole of the discipline is being in the right place when it sounds. The signal here is the water, and it moves on by an hour and nine minutes a day, so the Chain's clock drifts against everybody else's and against the sun. The virtue landed on the departure rather than the arrival, because the water does not punish you for being early.*

The gaps open for about seventy-five minutes either side of the low, and the low comes an hour and nine minutes later every day. So the Uvane day has never been tied to the sun, and the Chain wakes and sleeps at hours that walk round the clock.

You watch the water, you stand up, and you go before you are ready rather than after. Overstaying is the worst thing you can do to somebody, a host may never mention it, and that is the whole of Uvane manners. Miss the window and you are there until the next: most of a day, or when weather shuts the gaps, most of a season.

Because a sentence may not get finished, Uvane speech puts the load at the front. They sound abrupt to the point of rudeness everywhere else, are not being abrupt, and find the accusation strange.

Nobody wishes anybody well at a parting. You name the next window you expect to be back on, and if you do not know it you say so, and that is courteous.

**Look.** Humans, amphibioids and a good many Beluni, and asking which is which gets you a blank look. Nobody swims the gaps — sixteen knots through a hundred-metre channel takes an amphibioid apart the same as anybody — so the webbing and the gills buy nothing here and everyone knows it. A Beluni raised on the Chain and a Beluni off the Yarkan flats have a body in common and nothing else, and are mistaken for each other offworld constantly. What you notice is the bowl. Everyone carries their own, wooden or beaten tin, worn thin on one side from thirty years of being held the same way, because you eat where the water put you and that is almost never where you started.

**Names.** Two names, both given, neither by a parent. The first comes from whoever is waiting on the far side of your first solo crossing, usually between nine and fourteen. The second comes the same way from your first crossing of the deep gaps, which most people do around twenty and a good many never do at all, and having one name rather than two is not a thing anybody remarks on. Ronse Takhal, Ormo Bevu, Mira Kadu, Anu Serrin, Immeti, Dev. Offworlders assume the second name is a family name and ask about the Kadus, and get four different polite answers.

### How they regard each other

**The Beluni and the Zhurat** — *getting worse, and both sides can put a number on it*

Five hundred and eighty years ago somebody cut a bar at the head of the Bight to drain a stranded channel. Nobody maintains it. It has scoured itself wider ever since, and a wider throat is twenty-two minutes and two metres seventy at the Radna Wall — figures that were smaller when Venkovich measured them and will be larger when the people arguing now are dead. It kills one or two Zhurat a decade, always somebody who learned the count from a grandparent. Filling the cut drowns the drainage that makes the flats walkable and kills more Beluni than the bore ever has. Both peoples know both things. So they argue about the songs instead, and it gets loud.

**The Beluni and the Lohva** — *warming, and neither of them has noticed*

A cohort folds below fifteen, which puts unattached adults into the world at about forty-five, which is exactly when a Lohva household has a berth and a shelf going spare. For three hundred years most marriages on the lower Tuura have been Beluni and Lohva. Nobody arranges any of it and both peoples will tell you there is no pattern. What has changed is recent. A Beluni who married in used to lose the cohort name, and about forty years ago the river started keeping it anyway, as a fourth name form on a reach that already had three. Nobody decided this and nobody can name who began it. The Deshaal noticed and bring it up constantly.

**The Zhurat, the Lohva and the Deshaal** — *three-cornered — no two of them are the unit*

Everybody who leaves this planet leaves from the Qalin: a boat turn down the Tuura, then a named crossing of the salt. A Deshaal will not take an unnamed person out, because the carriers must know whose cost they are covering. A Zhurat's name is a patronymic from whoever taught them the ledges, two thousand kilometres behind them and not coming. So the Lohva who carried them says it — a river person doing a salt thing for a cliff person, the only place on the planet where any of the three does anything of the sort. Remove any one and the other two have no arrangement at all, which is why none of them thinks of it as one.

**The Deshaal and the Uvane** — *no relation of any kind*

Two and a half thousand kilometres, opposite hemispheres, and not one thing either has that the other wants. The Uvane close a parting by naming the window they expect to be back on. A Deshaal will stand in a room and wait for somebody else to say their name. Two peoples whose entire manners are built around arriving, neither of whom has ever arrived anywhere the other was. There is one recorded meeting, at a survey camp on the Qalin edge, and by every account it went perfectly well, and nothing came of it, and neither people has mentioned it since, because there is nothing to mention.

**The Beluni and the Uvane** — *cold, and nobody else on the planet can see it*

They are the only two peoples here who could have the conversation, and it goes badly every time. A Beluni thinks seventy-five minutes is a comfortable window and says so. A Uvane thinks walking out into the sea on purpose is the single stupidest thing anybody on Belune III does, and is right. They meet at the Tuura mouth every autumn for the shell trade, they are scrupulously polite, and there is a tightness to it that nobody from anywhere else on this planet can detect at all. The Lohva think the two of them are practically the same people and have said so, once each, to both.

**The Zhurat and the Uvane** — *admiration running one way only*

The hardest thing the Uvane do is leave on time, and a Zhurat cannot fail to. They are on the roof, they see the water, they go. Five hundred years of bird folk being effortlessly excellent at the one virtue the Chain is built around, without ever noticing there is a virtue involved. What the Uvane do instead is like the Wall's rock skins enormously — who cannot fly, who wait for the water like everybody else, and who get it wrong about as often as anyone does. Neither people has ever worked out why that friendship in particular is so easy.

### History

Five hundred and eighty years ago somebody cut through a bar at the head of the Bight to drain a channel that had stranded. It worked. Nobody has touched it since, and it has been scouring itself wider every year since.

Three hundred and eight years ago a Zhurat called Ostap Venkovich counted bore arrivals against the moon for thirty-one years, meaning to correct the teaching songs. What he proved instead was that the bore at the Radna Wall had come twenty-two minutes forward and two metres seventy up since the songs were made. Neither people has ever questioned the arithmetic. Nobody thanked him. He is buried above the Wall under a stone with the figure on it and nothing else.

Offworld contact arrived late, and by the only door there is. There is no harbour anywhere on this planet, and nothing sets down on a coast that walks seventy kilometres twice a day, so ships put down on the Qalin, which is flat, hard, dry and has no tide at all. The people who care least about the sea became the ones who meet everybody, and the galaxy's whole impression of Belune III was assembled by people who have never watched a tide come in.

Cohorts have been folding for as long as there have been cohorts, and a folded cohort scatters. There are Beluni in every port in reach and no Beluni settlement anywhere but the Bight, and nobody at home thinks either fact worth a remark.

### The Null

Nothing has come near it, and the way you can tell is the calendar.

The Bight tables run four hundred and forty years forward and the Chain's window list six hundred, both worked out by hand, both checked against the water at every low for as long as there have been lists. No entry in either has been found wrong by more than four minutes. A Beluni child learns lows that will happen when they are ninety. A Uvane parting names a window, and a fair number of those windows are decades out, and nobody treats this as a figure of speech.

That is the one thing Belune III has that *ARC 017 - Star Singer* does not. The catalogue can describe anything that exists and cannot describe an afternoon four hundred years from now.

Sevim Aydar keeps a Bight table in the drawer under the assay bench, unremarkably, the way anyone keeps a tide table. People come down and ask to look at it. Two of them are from Kessa. Sevim has never asked why, hands it over, and goes back to work, and it is the only thing they own that anyone has ever wanted to borrow.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- The cord. A Beluni pulls a knot through at each interval and can tell you to the minute how long they have been doing something without looking at anything. It makes them unnervingly good at four-hour procedures and liable to stand up in the middle of your sentence, because the cord ran out.
- What they will not teach. Ask a Beluni how to do anything they learned more than ten years ago and they will tell you who to ask instead. They are not being modest and they cannot be moved off it. The person they name is usually younger and is usually right.
- Twenty-two minutes. A Zhurat hands a time back short. Not as a correction, not with an explanation, not negotiably. It is correct in exactly one place in the universe.
- The number, said once. A Zhurat will commit out loud to a judgement about something they have only seen from four hundred metres away in bad light, and will then not revise it, because on the Wall revising it was never available. Sometimes they are wrong and they work the year anyway.
- Waiting to be named. A Deshaal will not say their own name. They will stand there. Say it for them and you have said something — they know exactly what, you almost certainly do not, and they will hold you to it for the rest of their life without ever raising it again.
- The next pair of hands. A Lohva will not let a thing leave a compartment until they know who is taking it — not where it ends up, the next pair of hands. They ask twice. They are not embarrassed about asking twice and do not understand why you are.

### Comedy

Amel spent seventeen years feeding arriving offworlders at the edge of the Qalin pan and describing the bore at the Radna Wall to them so vividly that a Census surveyor cited her by name in an entry — a name she had never once said to him, because somebody else had. When she found out about the citation she was mortified, went east at sixty-three to look at the thing properly, and came back reporting that it was rather smaller than she had been making it sound.

Three Beluni walking into a gallery drift apart to the full width of it and stay that way, because a bunched line loses two people instead of one. Step into the middle and they go round you and re-form at the same spacing. All three would deny it.

## Iseul III

*ordinary*

A warm, dark world under a red dwarf that flares with about forty minutes of notice, where the only geography that matters is what is over your head. Four peoples on four kinds of roof — cloud, water, limestone and nothing whatever — and the sharpest border on the planet is two hundred metres of ordinary ground where the caves stop.

### Geography

Iseul is a red dwarf. The third world sits close in — a ninety-four-day year, a thirty-one-hour day — and takes a temperate world's heat and a twelfth of its light. It is warm and it is dark. Leaf is black or deep burgundy: nothing to spare, nothing worth reflecting.

The star flares. Small ones come every few days and are a nuisance. A full white can run forty hours and will kill anything standing in it. The warning is that it gets darker: the disc thins and greys for between twenty-two and fifty-one minutes before the light arrives. On Iseul III the frightening thing is the dark.

So the only geography that matters is what is over your head. The map is a map of roofs.

The **Ondu**, equatorial, lies under a permanent cloud deck and a closed black canopy, a roof nobody built or owns. The **Ovren Shallows**, high and drowned, have water; a metre and a half of it stops a white, and the Ovren sit one out in chambers roofed by standing sea. The **Nalleun** is limestone, four thousand kilometres of solution cave under a bare surface. And the **Arrek** is a thousand kilometres of flat open ground with no roof whatever, and it is where the light is worth having.

The sharpest border on the planet is invisible. Where the limestone stops the cave stops with it, and two hundred metres of ordinary ground separates people always within reach of a door from people who are not.

### Peoples (4)

#### The Ossuan

**Ossuan (semi-human), and a shield-born minority of Ovren and Kaddow descent who hold figures like everybody else, because it is a protocol and not a biology** · The Nalleun — limestone karst in the mid-latitude interior. A bare, hot, fully exposed surface with four thousand kilometres of solution cave beneath it, running out under the northern sea at the drowned mouths and stopping dead at the Arrek edge

*Seed: Haudenosaunee title-raising: a name is an office that outlives its holders, and the holder's own identity is set aside on taking it. The terrain pushed it downward to every post on the shield rather than upward to a few, because the warning relay got there first and made a name out of a standing position. Shelter itself is not seeded on anything — a chamber holds so many bodies for so many hours, and the shares are that number divided up, which is arithmetic rather than custom.*

The shield is flat, hard and dense-aired, and sound carries. The warning goes by whistle, station to station, nineteen hundred metres a hop, and it has to outrun a person.

Four hundred and six figures make up the standing set, because that is about how many stay distinguishable at that range in wind. Ninety-odd are stations; the rest are functions. A figure belongs to the post and not the body at it, because a relay that broke every time somebody swapped out would be no relay.

So the figure is the name. Whoever is standing there answers to it; when they step down it means the next one, and the one before is a separate person in every sense the Ossuan care about.

Underground, shelter is held in shares against a chamber's number — so many bodies for so many hours, because air is arithmetic. Nobody is refused a door during a white, which makes the shares a polite fiction four times a year and an argument the rest of it. There are almost no instruments. Everything is whistled or sung.

**Look.** Tall, slender, hairless, silver-grey, eyes almost entirely pupil. The eyes are the dim-light adaptation and the reason a white blinds them. The colouring is not — dark would suit the ordinary light far better. Silver-grey reflects, and it matters for the four or five minutes in a lifetime anybody is caught out. Shield dress is layered, dull and unremarkable except at the throat, where everyone wears a plain stiffened collar to keep a hood off the ears. They go completely still while listening and have no idea they do it.

**Names.** No personal names. A post-name is a whistled figure with a spoken form; the spoken form crosses a table and the figure crosses the shield. The Census transcribes the spoken form or translates the function, which is why an Ossuan aboard ship is simply Navigator, and why Ossuan aboard find each other's translated names quietly grating and will not discuss it. Examples: **Ollunsae** (fourth call-post, Semmen line), **Hesullin** (holds the Hesull mouth, and so decides who comes in), **Renemmas** (cutting foreman, Renem passage), **Naeril** (counts bodies into the Ulle chamber), **Manneth** (keeps the Manne cistern), **Sae-Ondun** (speaker to the basin).

#### The Kaddow

**Rock skins, mostly — which is selection and not ancestry. Rock skin buys ninety seconds in the light and the Arrek kept whoever kept coming back. Humans and Ossuan-descended lines hold refuges too and nobody there thinks it worth a remark** · The Arrek — a thousand kilometres of flat open plain east of the Nalleun. No relief, no cover, no roof, and the richest ground on the planet for about a day after every white

*Seed: The Chinese lineage estate (zutian): land given permanently to a stated purpose rather than to any person, unsellable and undividable forever, with the management rotating among the founding line's branches in fixed order and the manager drawing a stated share. Here the endowment is a hole in the ground and the stated purpose is that the door opens, so the turn is counted in whites rather than years, and a keeper's name changes with the turn. The perennial dispute is not ownership, which nobody has, but whether the last branch handed it on fit.*

The root mat lies dead black for months; a white brings the whole plain up at once — forty hours of growth, a hard seed called kellet that keeps four years, and rot inside a day. The year's work happens the day after the light stops.

So everybody must be out there, and out there has no roof. The Arrek is a lattice of cut refuges, no working face more than eighteen minutes from a door, because the shortest warning anybody has recorded is twenty-two.

A refuge is not owned: the line that cut it gave it away to itself. It cannot be sold, filled or closed, and the door is never refused. What a line holds is the keeping, which passes among its branches in turns of eleven whites, so a Kaddow's second name changes on a schedule set by the star.

The skill nobody else has is calling the length; anyone can see the dip. Telling six hours of white from forty decides whether you finish the face or leave the tools, and it takes nine years.

**Look.** Rock skin in a single flat red-brown, the colour of one plain and nowhere else. A forearm that reads as one unbroken band means somebody who has never been anywhere, everybody can read it, and the Kaddow know. Boots resoled rather than replaced. They squint at nothing, constantly, out of thirty years of watching a star. A keeper wears no mark of it whatsoever, which visitors find maddening and which is the whole point — the refuge is not theirs, they are only the branch it is currently with.

**Names.** A given name and the refuge whose keeping their branch currently holds, so the second name changes when the turn passes — eleven whites, anywhere between two years and nine. Ennask Hevarn, Threll Bessa, Mirrek Onder, Osgeth Turrik, Sebba Aleth. A line between turns goes by the given name alone for those years — Ilmar, Nesk — which is entirely ordinary on the Arrek and which outsiders read as a demotion every single time.

#### The Onduwa

**Deep-sighted, mostly, with amphibioids and humans in the same villages and the same houses. Nobody there thinks the distinction organises anything** · The Ondu — equatorial lowland forest under a permanent cloud deck, south across the Arrek from everybody else

*Seed: Coast Salish potlatch: surplus you cannot store is converted into obligation held in other people's houses, and standing is measured in what is owed to you. Here the surplus is forty hours of fruit and nothing keeps in that heat, so the debt is denominated in the only genuinely scarce thing — hands, during the next white, on a date nobody can name.*

Under the cloud deck, ground light is thermal. The deep-sighted see by it. Everybody else works by touch, by water, and by somebody saying where things are, and this is so ordinary that the Onduwa do not think of themselves as a people with a sighted half and an unsighted half.

The canopy fruits under a full white and only while it lasts. Forty hours, then nothing, and the next might be nine days off or nineteen months. There is no calendar. There is a tally.

What a house holds is not stored food — nothing keeps in that heat — but hands owed: so many people for so many hours of the next white, promised by houses that ate yours. A long tally is comfort, a short one is work. Nobody there finds this precarious. What they find precarious is a rota, which assumes somebody knows when the work is coming.

They are the only people on the planet who have never had to run anywhere, and their word for shelter is their word for a house with people in it.

**Look.** The deep-sighted Onduwa have the wide flat cornea of people who see well into the heat end, and they do not blink much. Everybody dresses to be found by hand in the dark: cord and knotwork at the shoulder, different for every house, readable with two fingers. They touch each other constantly while talking and are baffled that this reads as anything.

**Names.** Everywhere on this planet the second name is the shelter your line holds rights in, and in the Ondu the shelter is a house full of people — so it is the house holding your line's largest standing tally, and it changes when the tally clears or moves, and it does. Wayo Duraa, Ijesa Kawal, Tamoro Sesso, Nkasi Mbenge, Oyelu Duraa, Sabene Kawal. A family carrying the same one for three generations is either very comfortable or in trouble, and there is a joke about not being able to tell from outside.

#### The Ovren

**Amphibioids and humans, and a steady leak of shield-born Ossuan who came down through the drowned mouths and found the light bearable** · The Ovren Shallows — a drowned high-latitude coast of tide flats and small islands north of the Nalleun, where the karst runs into the sea and the caves flood

*Seed: Norwegian and Faroese odal: land held inalienably by a kin line, unsellable outside it, with a right of redemption letting kin buy back what was sold for generations afterwards. There is no land here worth holding — the islands are small, wet and poor — so the odal attached to the tide-line and to the water you can get under when it goes, which are held as one thing and cannot be separated.*

Little photosynthesises this far north. What the Shallows eat comes up the current out of the Ondu weeks after a white, in a slow brown drift landing where the water decides.

A family holds a strand: a named stretch of tide-line, what the drift puts on it, and the downweir cut into it. A downweir is a chamber under a metre and a half of standing sea, held up on a stone rim, air beneath. The roof is water, and you can watch a white through it, green-white and moving, the only place on the planet anybody looks at one on purpose.

A strand cannot be sold outside the line. One sold under duress can be bought back by a cousin four generations on, which happens, and is most of Ovren law.

The drift depends on a flare over a forest nobody here can see, so the Ovren spent nine centuries trying to predict the white — not to shelter from it, which takes eleven seconds, but to know which beach to stand on. That is where the clock came from.

**Look.** Amphibioid Ovren carry the usual webbing and the faint forearm iridescence and are permanently damp, which here is simply the weather. Everybody wears the strand's knot somewhere visible and everybody can read everybody else's. They come up out of a downweir with salt dried in their hair and do not brush it out. They are the only people on the planet with a casual relationship to open sky — they stand about in it, at length, and it makes shield visitors twitch.

**Names.** A given name and the strand, which is the tide-line and the downweir and the water over your head, all in one word. Halvi Ovreth, Marrin Nysk, Anse Hadrel, Erlask Tilven, Nivenne Sevreth, Kalder Immen. The strand is the inheritance and the name at once. A line that loses its strand keeps using the name for about two generations and then stops, and there is no polite way to ask which stage a family is at.

### How they regard each other

**The Ossuan and the Ovren** — *old, close, and physically overlapping*

North of the shield the karst runs into the sea and floods, and the drowned mouths are where the two roofs overlap: a chamber a shield line holds a share in, which an Ovren swims into from underneath. Traffic both ways for as long as anyone counted, heavy intermarriage, and standing credit between families who have not met in three generations and know each other's names anyway. The cost is upstairs. An Ovren has never had to think about where they are, because at home the roof is everywhere, and every few years one of them is out on open limestone at the wrong hour. The shield stopped trying to explain and refuses to let anybody start after midday.

**The Ossuan and the Kaddow** — *working perfectly well, and privately costing one side something*

Nothing grows on limestone, so the shield eats Arrek kellet, and the Arrek's refuges are cut by shield crews, because the Ossuan have been cutting rock for four hundred years and nobody else has. A cutting crew on open ground is twelve near-blind people who cannot hear a whistle, so every crew gets a Kaddow keeper whose whole season is telling them when to stop. Nobody pays for that. It comes out of the refuge's turn. Every cutter knows one Kaddow's name, and at the end of a season the cutter takes a new figure and the person the keeper kept is gone. The Kaddow have a word for it — ossendeth — and no Ossuan has ever asked what it means.

**The Kaddow and the Ovren** — *going, and nobody is angry about it*

For nine centuries an Ovren boat needing to know whether a white would run six hours or forty sent to the Arrek and paid a caller in salt and rope. Halvi Ovreth's clock read the dip. The one built twenty-two years ago guesses the length off its shape and is right seven times in ten. A good caller is right nineteen times in twenty and takes nine years to make. Seven in ten is instant, costs nothing, sits in every boat, and for a boat it is plenty. So the paying stopped, then the apprenticeships, and there are four people left who can call a length. Nobody is angry. The Ovren are proud of the clock, and right to be.

**The Ossuan and the Onduwa** — *mutual non-registration*

A thousand kilometres of roofless ground lies between them and neither can cross it, so neither has ever had to have an opinion. The standing set contains a figure for the basin; it was compiled six hundred years ago and nothing has needed it since. The shield keeps a post called Sae-Ondun, speaker to the basin, filled without a break for two hundred years and never once used for anything. The Onduwa do not know the post exists. Neither side finds any of this strange, because neither side has thought about it.

**The Ovren and the Onduwa** — *recent, improving, and mortifying on both sides*

On Iseul the sea is the short way and flat ground the wall, so the two peoples furthest apart are the ones getting to know each other. The current runs north, so an Ovren year turns on a flare over a forest none of them had seen. Then the clock told them when the drift would come, somebody worked back to where it came from, and in thirty years the run south became ordinary. It is going well and it is excruciating. The Ovren arrive with a date and a list, at houses with no calendar, which measure everything in hands owed and cannot work out what to enter for a guest who will not be there for the white.

### History

Eleven hundred years ago a white ran forty-one hours. The Nalleun had been surface-farming under sheeted awnings; it stopped being able to inside a week, and four centuries of digging followed. Every chamber on the shield is younger than that white, and so is the share system, and so is the habit of counting bodies at a door.

Seven hundred years ago shield crews went out onto the Arrek after kellet, which keeps, and which limestone cannot grow. They cut the first eleven refuges and people stayed to keep them. The ones whose bodies took the light stayed longest, which is why the Arrek is mostly rock skin now — selection, not ancestry, and a Kaddow will correct you on that before you have finished the sentence.

Six hundred years ago the whistled sets were standardised into the four hundred and six. It was a compilation of a dozen local sets, and about ninety figures were dropped for sitting too close to others at range. A figure is a name, and a dropped figure is a line of ancestors who no longer have one. The families concerned have not let it go, and it surfaces every time a new figure is proposed, which is roughly once a generation and takes years.

Sixty-one years ago Halvi Ovreth built a clock to time her boats. Twenty-two years ago somebody built one that guesses the length.

The Census first called a hundred and thirty years back. It calls less now.

### The Null

Nothing has happened to Iseul III. The problem is the way there.

The lane thinned nine years ago and stopped — one of the eleven, and one of the four Navigator can still sing, which she does not mention. The way round is three months instead of eleven days, so the Census has called twice in nine years where it used to call yearly.

The specific loss is the Ondu. The canopy fruits only during a full white — forty hours, no calendar, no way to predict the next — so surveying it means holding a ship in orbit for up to nineteen months waiting for the star to do something. Nobody can hold a ship there for nineteen months at the end of a three-month run. The largest annual biological event in this arm has been observed properly once, by a party of six, forty years ago, and the entry runs four pages.

Day to day it is families. A Kaddow daughter who shipped out for three years is going on seven, and the keeping has passed her branch twice without her in it.

The shield does not really believe in the Null, on the grounds that nothing on the shield has changed, and nothing on the shield has changed in four hundred years. Ossuan aboard have mostly stopped trying to explain it.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- Will not let a door close with somebody on the other side of it. Nobody on the shield is refused a door during a white, and it is not a courtesy, it is the one absolute. A shield-raised crewmate will put a hand into a closing bulkhead during a drill and be entirely unembarrassed about it afterwards.
- Sleeps twice. A thirty-one-hour day makes biphasic sleep ordinary and nobody from Iseul III has ever successfully converted. They take the second sleep in four-hour pieces wherever the rota leaves a gap, and are permanently, mildly out of phase with the whole ship.
- Holds hundreds of near-identical tones and tells them apart under noise — four hundred and six figures learned from the age of three, in wind, across nineteen hundred metres. It is why so many navigators come from here. It is also why a shield-raised engineer will say a manifold's pitch has moved, be unable to say by how much, and be right.
- Stops dead at a brightening rather than at a dark, which is the opposite of everyone else's reflex. A welding arc, a bloom coming up, the emergency lighting test: the body halts first and then decides. Ovren-raised crew do not do this at all — they turn and look at it, because at home you watch a flare through the roof. Put one of each in a compartment and you can tell which ground they came off.
- Keeps a running tally of hands owed in both directions and never mentions it. A basin-raised crewmate volunteers for everything, constantly, and is not being generous — they are storing it, because an obligation is the only thing in the Ondu that keeps. Nobody aboard knows they are being counted.
- Holds an open doorway about three seconds longer than anybody needs. Rock skin buys ninety seconds in the light, and an Arrek keeper spends them on being the last one in, every time, out of a habit with no application whatever on a ship and which has never once been dropped.

### Comedy

A village near the Hesull mouth taught a six-year-old the warning figure properly for the first time, and she blew it correctly, at dawn, for practice. Two hundred people were underground before breakfast with their tools left where they lay. Whoever blew it is a cutting foreman at the far end of the Renem passage now, and is a different person, and it is still the first thing anybody says about her. She has developed a very economical way of getting in front of it.

Separately: an Ovren woman married onto the shield and named her daughter, which shield people find frankly eccentric — naming a thing before you know what it does. The village used the name for eleven years and mispronounced it, all four hundred of them identically wrong, and nobody told her. The daughter took a figure at twelve and was audibly relieved, and still uses the wrong version with her Ovren relatives because she prefers it.

## Tannhul VI

*ordinary*

Tannhul VI is 2.4 g with a thirty-one hour day, and both run through everything on it: rivers are the roads, a watershed is a wall with no door in it, and a fall from standing is a medical event. Six peoples share it, none of them can reach more than two of the others without a boat or a very long walk, and every one of them carries a river as a second name.

### Geography

Surface gravity is 2.4 g and the day is thirty-one hours, so everyone sleeps twice and a fall from standing is a medical event. Highest ground anywhere: 1,205 m, and it is a worn stub.

Rivers are the roads. Nobody at 2.4 g carries a boat over anything, so a watershed is a wall with no door in it.

The **Lumbaya Basin** is most of the inhabited world: hot, wet, braided, 2.9 bar at the floor, nothing visible past three kilometres. Seventy-four kilometres of its lower course is **the Fold**, where the channel re-lays itself every flood; a barge takes thirteen days and needs a pilot. Below it lies **the Jandu**: shallow warm reaches, the densest living stone in reach.

North-west the **Ombe**, a Lumbaya tributary, passes within nine kilometres of the **Vurra**, which runs to a different sea. The divide is sixty-one metres. That gap is **the Carry**, the only hole in any wall here.

North-east the basin fails into **the Pelan Flats**: 1,450 km of white evaporite, the only ground in direct sun, because that air holds no water to filter it. South, the **Bellain Coast** runs 900 km under permanent fog off a cold current. West the ground tilts up 900 m over 400 km — no scarp, a long rise — and its rivers are unnavigable.

Ice at both poles. The ley current beneath is one long unvarying note, which is why attuned Tannhul engineers spend their first year aboard seasick.

### Peoples (6)

#### The Kalunde

**Rock skin, with Tesk and amphibioids** · The Lumbaya Basin — the braided lowland rivers and the slack-water bars along them

*Seed: Nguni ukusisa: stock is loaned out to another household, which keeps the yield and a share of the increase while the animal itself stays the lender's, so a household's wealth is distributed through other people's homesteads and is invisible in its own. Tannhul substitutes living stone for cattle, which moves the clock from years to decades and makes the whole arrangement slower than a lifetime.*

Living stone grows on the silt bars in slack water, and it grows for decades. A house with more stock than bed places the surplus out. The receiving house works it and keeps a stated fraction of the growth; the parent stock stays the placer's until it is called back.

So a house's wealth sits in other people's water and cannot be counted by looking at it. The largest bed on a reach may be working almost nothing of its own. What a house is admired for is how well it grows stock that is not theirs — a reputation built over thirty years and lost in one bad decade. Calling a placement back early is ordinary, permitted, and says a thing about the grower that nobody wants said, so it happens rarely and the house that does it is careful for a generation afterwards.

Rock skin sinks. Every working bank on the Lumbaya is railed, every bar crew works on a line rated to 5.2 tonnes, and the Kalunde who are not rock skin wear the line too.

**Look.** Mostly rock skin, and Tannhul rock skins are long-boned and enormously heavy — tall, thick through the neck and shoulder, slow to start. A Kalunde in a one-gravity corridor takes up more room than anyone expects and has to be careful about it. Not everyone is built the same: the beds also run to Tesk, who get inside a stock frame where the clearance is a hand's width, and to the amphibioids who go under the bars.

Basin silt lays down in a rock skin as a dark basalt grey with a green cast, and because it is the first environment anyone works it is the lowest band on the forearm and the widest. A Kalunde who left at twenty and has worked four worlds since has that band under a great deal else and is genuinely hard to place, which the ones who left find easier than the ones who did not.

They do not gesture above the shoulder. Lifting an arm costs something, so the talking happens in the face and the low hands.

**Names.** A personal name and a river. The river is not where you were born — it is the reach where the family's oldest placement sits, which is usually somebody else's water a few hundred kilometres away. So a Kalunde is named for a river where they own something they are not allowed to touch. When the oldest placement moves, everybody in the house is renamed in an afternoon, nobody makes an occasion of it, and it happens about twice a century.

The personal name is given by the house holding that placement. They choose it, do not say it in advance, and have to come downriver to give it, at first standing — around two and a half years here, which nobody thinks is late. The milk-name is not used again. Ojo Mbeki, Adaeze Runsau, Femi Ondaba, Kesiwe Iwerre, Nnenna Aluweh, Bolaji Shonde. Ojo Mbeki has been on the Mbeki twice.

#### The Culven

**Human, Tesk, amphibioid and rock skin** · The Jandu — the shallow reaches below the Fold, lived on afloat

*Seed: Carolinian canoe-house membership: you belong to a house of the hull rather than to a piece of land, membership is inherited, and it carries both an obligation of labour and an absolute claim on shelter from any hull of the house. Tannhul took the land out of it entirely, because the Culven cannot live on it.*

They came on a Verekh labour contract a hundred and seventy-three years ago, to work reaches no Kalunde house had the beds for. The contract ended. The passage home ended with it.

A body not built for 2.4 g does not adapt, it breaks. A Culven human loses about five centimetres of height by fifty-five and their feet go long before that. So the Culven live afloat, where buoyancy does what bone will not. Bed work runs in relays of ninety minutes by hull, and nobody is on the silt longer if anyone is counting, which somebody always is. Infants spend most of two years in shallow pens, because a human baby at 2.4 g cannot safely hold up its own head.

Every hull keeps a bunk for whoever comes off a ship — not for a member, for anyone. It is the oldest thing the Culven have and the one they would be most surprised to be praised for.

A hull is repaired plank by plank until nothing original is left, and it is the same hull.

**Look.** Humans, Tesk, amphibioids and a scattering of rock skin, because a recruitment off worn-out worlds takes whoever will sign. The humans are comprehensively marked by living somewhere that does not want them: short, thick through the ankle, and past fifty a stick or a frame ashore and nothing whatever afloat. The Tesk are the least troubled people on the planet and know it. The amphibioids are the ones who go down.

The cap is what outsiders notice. Padded, close-fitting, worn from about two, because a fall from standing at 2.4 g takes the back of the head. A Culven Tesk wears one and does not need one. A Culven at one gravity still wears it, has never once needed it, and would feel undressed without it.

**Names.** The given name does the work. No name is used twice on a hull while its holder is alive, and a name comes back into the pool when it leaves; children go by a milk-name until one frees up, which can be a season or most of a childhood, and plenty of Culven answer to both for life.

The second name is the channel the hull was moored in the night you were born. The Jandu re-lays itself every flood, so most of those channels are gone, and a Culven's river is generally water that does not run anywhere. Nobody finds this poetic. They find it an addressing problem, because four hulls can be Vaus for the same dead channel and the post goes wrong. Petra Halloran, Joris Vaus, Anneke Dunmore, Joss Brannick, Ilse Sorrell, Teodor Vrancke. Hulls have names too — the Wick, the Pallis, the Garron — and those are addresses, not part of anybody's name.

#### The Zhemet

**Sunfed and rock skin, with humans and Tesk on the night watch** · The Pelan Flats — the evaporite pan and the rim towns along its southern edge

*Seed: Roman negotiorum gestio: you may act on an absent person's behalf without their instruction, but having begun you must carry it through, you are answerable for what you spoil by meddling, and you recover your exact costs and earn nothing. The Pelan pushed it to its hard edge, because sunfed dormancy cannot be scheduled and a third of any district is absent, without notice, at any hour.*

A dew-field is eighteen thousand dark stones on a fall to a cistern. They take the day's heat, lose it after dark, and the condensation runs down and in. Two generations to lay one, because every stone is carried.

Sunfed dormancy cannot be scheduled. A house declares the window it expects to go under in and is wrong half the time, so a third of any district is absent without notice. The Zhemet rule for acting on an absent person's behalf: you are never obliged to begin; once begun you must finish; you take back your exact costs and not one stone more. Half-helping and leaving it is the worst thing a Zhemet does.

Slow to start, impossible to stop. A Zhemet will look at a neighbour's collapsed cistern for three days before touching it, which off the pan reads as coldness and is the opposite.

Water is outside the rule. You may not refuse it to anyone on the Pelan, and water given is not recoverable, so a household that empties its cistern being decent has emptied its cistern.

**Look.** Sunfed, mostly — broad and flat-framed, dark green over the upper surfaces and much paler beneath. The pan is the only ground on Tannhul that gets direct light, and there is no sunfed anywhere else on the planet.

Also rock skin, in numbers, because the pan is hot, bright, abrasive and perfectly comfortable if your skin is mineral. Pelan rock skins lay down a salt-white first band, faintly pink at the joints, which is as far from the basin grey as one species gets. Two Tannhul rock skins standing together look like different animals and are not.

The dew is harvested in the dark. A sunfed is no use at night, so most households run both watches, and the watches are not inherited — a sunfed child of night people works days because of what they are, and thinks nothing of it.

**Names.** A personal name and a fossil channel. The pan's old watercourses show as pale seams in the evaporite, dry for tens of thousands of years, and they are ordinary addresses: the Sedra, the Oyun, the Halek, the Nurram. Zhemet find nothing sad in this and are puzzled that Culven do.

Each district works from a small stock of name-beginnings, and the beginning says which flood you were born between, so Zhemet of an age have names that start alike and can place a stranger's age within a couple of years on hearing it. The stock comes round again about every second lifetime. Ansei Sedra, Anvero Halek, Kerani Oyun, Kerovo Nurram, Dovash Sedra, Jelane Oyun.

#### The Nembai

**Rock skin and oni, with Tesk** · The Carry — nine kilometres of laterite divide between the Ombe and the Vurra, and the stage towns at both ends

*Seed: Tibetan ula: transport corvée owed to the road rather than to any person. Households along a stage furnish carriage on demand to whoever arrives with the right entitlement, the load is handed over at a fixed boundary to the next stage, and the obligation cannot be discharged in money. What makes it hard is that it is triggered by somebody turning up.*

The Carry is cut into eleven stages of about eight hundred metres, because eight hundred metres is how far a person goes under forty kilos at 2.4 g before the load comes down. A load crosses in two days and changes hands eleven times.

Each stage keeps a roster. Your turn comes round about every ninth day and you never know the hour, because it comes when somebody arrives. Three things cross for nothing: water, seed stock, and a person who cannot walk. Everything else pays. What counts as seed stock has been argued here for four hundred years and both sides of it are reasonable.

A household called who does not come is not fined. The load waits, everyone can see whose turn it was, and that is the whole of the enforcement.

Nobody hands anything to anybody. A Nembai hand-over is a set-down: the load goes on the ground and the next stage lifts it clean. Passing a weight hand to hand at this gravity is how shoulders are destroyed, and the habit does not come off.

**Look.** Rock skin heavily, oni in numbers, and Tesk, who do not carry and who get inside a loaded cradle to check a lashing where nobody else can reach. On the Carry those are the same job and nobody has ever thought otherwise.

The divide is old laterite, the only red ground on Tannhul, so a Nembai rock skin lays down a first band of iron ochre that no other rock skin on the planet has. It reads as rust and people ask.

Every Nembai past fifteen carries the same callus across the top of the shoulder and the upper back from the cradle straps, including the ones who left at sixteen and have not carried anything since.

**Names.** A personal name and two rivers, in the order you carry them. Which way round is set by the end of the Carry your stage sits on — Ombe-Vurra from the Lumbaya side, Vurra-Ombe from the other — and the Nembai are the only people on Tannhul with two rivers in their name. Off-world they get asked about it constantly; the answer takes eleven seconds and disappoints everybody. Rathe Ombe-Vurra, Halden Vurra-Ombe, Miren Ombe-Vurra, Odeno Vurra-Ombe, Sulka Ombe-Vurra, Jesuo Vurra-Ombe.

#### The Sevrin

**Feline and amphibioid, with humans and Made** · The Bellain Coast — nine hundred kilometres of fogged southern shore and the hundred and six short rivers that cut it

*Seed: Andean mit'a: a turn of labour assessed on the community rather than the household, allocated internally by the community, served as time and not goods, with those who stay provisioning those who go. On the Bellain the undertaking is sound rather than stone, most of the people who owe turns have never been in a boat, and a turn is forty days of striking a bell.*

The fog does not lift. Two hundred and forty marks stand along nine hundred kilometres of coast, each a hung stone struck on its own count — the Corrow on eleven, the Emmet on seventeen, primes so the patterns never line up. A boat places itself by the interval between two.

A turn is forty days, two to a mark, because one person striking alone loses the count by the third week. Villages are assessed in turns rather than households, the village decides who goes, and their year's food is settled before they leave. There is no honour in it. It is a turn.

Most Sevrin who owe turns have never been to sea. Felines hold a count through weather that defeats everyone else, so feline strikers get the marks that matter most, which is not a promotion but a longer walk over worse ground.

Whether a Made at a mark discharges a turn was settled here eighty years ago. It does: a turn is time a person gave up. The Sevrin are mildly surprised this is hard anywhere else.

**Look.** Pale, all of them — nobody on this coast has stood in direct sun. Felines in numbers, whose double coat never entirely dries and who have stopped mentioning it; amphibioids, who go down to clear the mark moorings in water at four degrees; humans; Made.

Everyone carries a counting cord: a knotted line, forty knots, worked through the fingers to hold a count while the hands and the eyes are doing something else. Sevrin who have not been near a mark in thirty years cannot hold a conversation without one, and a Sevrin who has mislaid theirs will have a bootlace knotted inside a minute and will not have noticed doing it.

**Names.** A personal name and a river. There are a hundred and six named rivers on the Bellain and the longest is thirty-one kilometres, so the name is extremely precise — the smallest river has forty people on it. Two Sevrin meeting off-world place each other within a kilometre on the first exchange and then say so, at length, which everybody else finds abrupt. Osme Corrow, Idrenne Emmet, Varlo Tilsen, Sabbe Nune, Hesken Sarragh, Elu Vell.

#### The Ardu

**Rock skin, human and oni, and most of the planet's deep-sighted** · The western heads — the long rise, its short cold rivers, and the landing field

*Seed: The commenda: a single venture, capital from one party and labour and travel from the other, the split fixed before departure, and loss of the goods falling on the capital alone — the traveller loses their labour and owes nothing. The venture is fully accounted at the end and then stops existing. Sixty to ninety days on ground where one crossing takes three pushed the Ardu to the far end of it.*

On the heads the rivers are fast, cold and full of rock, and no boat goes up them, so the Ardu are the only people on Tannhul who walk between rivers as a matter of course. Everyone downstream regards it as impossible.

Nobody carries their own goods. A party of nine going out for eighty days carries eleven separate ventures, each with its own backer and its own ratio agreed before anyone left, and the accounting at the end takes about as long as a day's walk. The ratio is argued ferociously beforehand and never once afterwards.

If a crossing goes and the load is in the Oben, that is the backer's loss. The carrier walks home owing nothing and goes out again in spring. So the Ardu are the only people here who do not carry a bad year forward, and everyone else on the planet finds this either enviable or slightly obscene.

The landing field is on the heads, because it is the only clear air on Tannhul. It is four hundred kilometres and four crossings from anywhere.

**Look.** Rock skin banding a hard blue-grey shot with a bright fleck, which is granite and which no other Tannhul rock skin has; humans; oni; and most of the planet's deep-sighted, because the heads are the only place where seeing past three kilometres is any use at all — in the basin the haze defeats even them.

Weathered across the cheekbones, from the only real sun anybody gets outside the pan. Everybody walks with a staff and nobody puts it down, indoors either. At 2.4 g the staff is a third leg, and a third leg is the difference between a stumble and a hip.

**Names.** A personal name and the river you were first carried across, which was somebody else's decision and which you cannot change. There is no family name at all. Ardu households move up and down the heads, so a family can run through five rivers in three generations and nobody thinks of them as related by name. Bereth Sedran, Ansul Kerreth, Ollun Oben, Torvan Ashwe, Esseli Drin, Marokh Sedran.

### How they regard each other

**The Kalunde, the Culven and the Ardu** — *a three-way tangle in which no pair is the unit*

All three handle other people's property and all three answer the question of whose loss it is differently. Kalunde placed stock stays the placer's forever, so a bad decade is the grower's reputation. A Culven hull that loses a load makes it good, because the hull is the member and the member is what the house is. An Ardu venture puts the whole loss on the backer and the carrier walks away clean. Kalunde stock now goes down the Fold on Culven hulls under Ardu backing, forty-odd times a year, and any two of the three can agree while the third's answer still has to be paid. The arrangement at Ibare that covers it has been temporary for ninety years.

**The Kalunde and the Culven** — *an old grievance, currently and unmistakably improving*

The Kalunde surveyed the Jandu reaches and declined them: silted, unholdable, no slack water a bed would sit in. Which is where the contract's three thousand went, and the reaches turned out to grow the densest stock on the planet. The Kalunde case: they let go of the best ground on Tannhul. The Culven case: they only got it because it was worthless. What they actually argue about is the pilot's fraction through the Fold, renegotiated eight times in living memory. The ninth went differently: somebody named a number both sides privately thought slightly unfair to themselves, and both took it. The Jandu now holds more placed Kalunde stock than the basin does, which both peoples are the last to work out.

**The Nembai and the Ardu** — *slowly getting worse, and nobody has done anything wrong*

An Ardu party is already walking. Going round the Carry is forty kilometres instead of nine, over the divide on foot, and it means no eleven hand-overs and no argument about whether the load counts as seed stock. Each party that goes round is a small thing. Over thirty years it has become most of them. The stages at the Vurra end now go nine days between loads where they used to go two, and the roster at stage eleven has quietly become a list of people who are not called. The Nembai are not being denied anything they are owed. They are owed something nobody is asking for, which turns out to be worse.

**The Kalunde and the Nembai** — *a working arrangement both sides are oddly sentimental about and neither will say so*

Living stone will not travel. It comes off the bar wet, in a cradle, and has about thirty hours before it is dead weight, which is just inside one Tannhul day and almost exactly what the Carry takes with the stages running hard. Every Kalunde bed on the Vurra — there are ninety-one — was founded by stock that went over inside a day, and the Nembai know which stages carried which bed and will tell you at length. It is the only load on the Carry that is not a turn. The roster is suspended, half of stage six turns out at any hour, and nobody on either side has ever put a reason to this.

**The Zhemet and the Ardu** — *a flat refusal neither side thinks is unreasonable*

Dew-fields want dense dark stone and the pan has none. The Ardu have granite, which is the right stone and lasts, and the Ardu will not move goods on anything but a venture, where the backer bears the loss. The Zhemet cannot buy on those terms. A Zhemet who begins must finish, and a stone lying somewhere in the Oben is a thing begun and not finished, and no amount of explaining has ever made that a survivable thought. So almost no Ardu stone reaches the pan. Both peoples can state the other's position accurately, neither thinks the other is wrong, and they argue about it with real pleasure whenever they meet.

**The Zhemet and the Culven** — *complete mutual reliance and no relationship whatsoever*

So the pan buys dead bed-stone instead. Culven crews cut it out of the reaches, pole it the length of the Lumbaya and go over in the flood window; it crumbles in about forty years, which is fine, because a dew-field is laid over two generations and a stone can be replaced. Something like nine hundred Culven spend six weeks on the rim every year and have done for a hundred and forty. They know the rim towns well. The rate per hundred stone has not moved in that time and nobody counts the load at either end. Neither people has a word for the other beyond the pan and the stone boats.

**The Zhemet and the Sevrin** — *no contact at all, and a friendship that exists only on ships*

Four thousand kilometres apart, no river between them, no trade and no dispute. A Zhemet has no particular reason to have heard of the Bellain and mostly has not. What happens off-world is that they find each other immediately, because both count aloud without noticing — stones on one side, strikes on the other — and each finds the other's counting restful in a way neither can explain and both have stopped trying to. Berth allocation on three separate ships has quietly started putting them together. On Tannhul this relationship does not exist.

### History

Three things; the rest is arrangement.

**The Overtop, three hundred and twelve years ago.** The Lumbaya came over its north bank in the wrong place and ran across the southern Pelan for a season, taking over half the dew-fields there. The relaying took fifty-eight years, and in the course of it a great many people began work on an absent neighbour's field and did not finish, each for a good reason. What came out of it is the exact stone-count the Zhemet keep now, and the fact that an unfinished start is still the sharpest shame on the pan.

**The Ombe Cut, two hundred and forty years ago.** Somebody proposed to dig a canal across the Carry's nine kilometres. At 2.4 g the digging is not the problem; the spoil is. Everything taken out must be carried up and out, and wet laterite slumps. Nineteen years, one thousand four hundred metres, six deep. The trench is still there, holds water, and the Nembai grow things in it. *Cutting the Ombe* is what anyone here calls a doomed effort.

**The contract, a hundred and seventy-three years ago.** Verekh III wanted living-stone hull stock grown at 2.4 g. The Kalunde beds could not scale: a bed needs slack water and it was all held. So the yards recruited off several worn-out worlds and landed three thousand people, in seven landings, in the part of the river nobody wanted. Then the contract ended, the ships stopped, and the three thousand were still there.

### The Null

Nothing on Tannhul has changed except who arrives.

The field on the heads used to take nine ships a year and, with them, a trickle of people — thirty or so, married in, contracted in, or came off a ship and did not get back on. It is how Tannhul got the Culven and it is how it has got everybody else since. Seven years ago it became two ships, and then it became ships whose people do not get off. Nothing else moved. The beds are full, the stock is growing, the stone rate is what it was, and the marks are struck on the count.

Three things cross the Carry for nothing, and the third is a person who cannot walk. That clause is four hundred years old and it exists for people off ships, who arrive at 2.4 g with the wrong bones and cannot manage four hundred kilometres and four crossings. Stage six keeps a cradle rated for a person. It is inspected twice a year and has not been used in seven.

Every Culven hull keeps a bunk for whoever comes off a ship — not for a member, for anyone. There are something over two hundred hulls in the Jandu. The bunks are made up and turned, nobody has converted one, and the Culven born since have never seen one used.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- Does not catch anything. At 2.4 g a thing that has begun to fall has already landed, so what gets taught instead is not to be under it and not to move suddenly, and both go in before anyone can walk. At one gravity it reads as slow, or as not having noticed. They noticed. They stepped back.
- Can tell you what a crate weighs from lifting one corner, within a couple of kilos, every time. Being wrong about a mass at 2.4 g is an injury, so the calibration goes in early and never comes out. They cannot do it on request, because asking makes them think about it.
- Gives you a number when you asked how they are, and is counting under their breath the rest of the time. A Kalunde answers with how many beds their placed stock is sitting in. A Zhemet gives what they are up or down in stones this season. A Sevrin is on a count that started before you came in and will still be running when you leave. Nobody has explained any of it in years and nobody aboard asks any more.
- Writes letters for about a fortnight and then stops for the year. On Tannhul the flood window is the only time a boat crosses the Pelan, so the whole correspondence happens in one push. There is no flood aboard and no boat. It still happens in that fortnight, and it is mostly about stock.
- One mark from whichever people raised them. A Kalunde clips on to a walkway that already has a rail and gives a reason that is not the reason. A Culven still wears the padded cap at one gravity. An Ardu will not put the staff down indoors. A Nembai cannot hand you anything — the cup goes on the table between you, every time, for nine years. A Zhemet who has begun something small on your behalf will still be at it a fortnight later and cannot be talked out of it.
- Crosses a gallery to take hold of somebody who has stumbled, and asks twice. On Tannhul a trip is a broken hip. The second ask is the part people remember.

### Comedy

The windowsill in Adaeze Runsau's front room at Nsende carries seventeen potted plants from nine worlds, all staked, splinted and guyed with line, all alive. Nothing on Tannhul grows upright, so visitors keep arriving with something tall, and she rigs every one of them properly over an afternoon with great seriousness. She does not like a single one of them. She has said so to two people and both assumed she was being modest.

Osme Corrow came aboard off the Bellain and found the galleries impossible — mist, moss, no horizon, every junction the same green — so over about a year she quietly put in a count she could place herself by: a spanner against a pipe on eleven at the third-deck junction, another on seventeen at the aft stair, worked round as far as her cabin. She did not mention it to anyone, because on the Bellain that is simply what you do about not knowing where you are. Ilka Voressen walks the spine twice a shift with a tapping-hammer, two taps, a pause, two taps, and spent six weeks convinced she had a sympathetic resonance building somewhere in the third-deck run. She took a bulkhead down. Osme, who had been carefully working around Ilka's hammer for a month because she thought the ship had an intermittent, helped her take it down. They worked it out on the second day of the reassembly and neither has fully recovered. The count is still there. Ilka added two of the marks herself.

## Vantage

*city*

Vantage is a world with the outdoors built over: four kilometres dug down, nine stacked up, and forty-three billion people living in the thickness, where distance is measured in days of staging rather than kilometres. Everyone carries a number after their name, everyone will tell you it is only a breathing rating, and everyone knows perfectly well what else it is.

### Geography

Vantage measures distance in days. Across is nothing — a hundred kilometres of roofed city is a two-hour ride. Up is six days, because that is how long a body takes to stage; an unstaged city body is useless at Seventy inside an hour and dead by the second day. Fewer than a million people a year make the trip, out of forty-three billion.

Thirteen kilometres of stack, with the old ground in the middle at Naught.

Below it, four kilometres of rock that is hot before anyone touches it. The runs are worked at fifty-five degrees and streaming wet, because water is how heat leaves a planet with a lid on. At Under-Thirty a pinhole will take a finger off.

Naught to Forty is the roofed city, four kilometres of continuous interior holding most of everybody. No wind, no season, no sky — and weather anyway, the stack's own heat climbing and turning over twice a day, which everyone down there reads without knowing they are reading it.

Forty to Forty-Five is cloud. The build stands in it. Everything above drinks what condenses there and everything below is rained on from the inside.

Forty-Five to Ninety is open rigging in thin dry air, blinding by day, forty below at night. Nothing dropped up here lands nearer than four kilometres down, on somebody.

Two load decks of three hundred solid metres carry all of it, and a hole through one is a debt against the structure. Hence fourteen through-shafts on the entire world.

### Peoples (4)

#### The Ilmeth

**Mixed, like everywhere. Bird folk and sunfed are over-represented — hollow bones and a body fed on light are both cheap in kilograms — alongside humans, angels, Tesk and a dozen semi-human peoples. Being bird folk does not make you Ilmeth: there are more bird folk in the buried city than in the whole canopy, and most of them have never flown and would not know how to begin. What the canopy does, it does to every body in it. Ilmeth lungs run half again the volume of a city body's, and an Ilmeth sheds heat very badly, so thirty degrees is a medical problem regardless of species.** · The canopy — Forty-Five to Ninety, open rigging above the weather

*Seed: The perpetual endowment of Ottoman and Levantine law: an asset alienated forever to a stated purpose, administered by an heritable line who draw a stipend and can never own, sell or divide it, with the founder's deed governing after everyone involved is dead — and with substitution, the exchange of an endowed asset for another of exactly equal value, as the single fraught escape hatch. Vantage keeps the whole apparatus and changes the unit of value to kilograms standing in air.*

A mast is stood to a charge. Somebody builds it, writes down what it is for, and puts it permanently out of the mass ledger — it cannot be sold, divided, borrowed against or repurposed by anybody ever, including the family that raised it. What that family gets instead is the keeping. They rig it, re-stay it every ninety years, and draw a living allowance off it in kilograms, generation after generation, owning not one gram.

Most charges are dull. Some are not. A charge written six hundred years ago may require a mast to light one shaft two kilometres below, and if the shaft has gone dark the mast lights it anyway, because the charge does not care.

The only way out is exchange: moving a charge onto another structure of exactly equal standing mass, which requires the assent of every keeper on the reach and is read aloud over two days. There have been forty-one exchanges in six hundred years. Any Ilmeth can name all forty-one.

**Look.** Barrel-chested whatever the species, and wind-burned in a specific pattern across the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose from working the outer spars. Layered undyed fibre, because dye is mass and mass is somebody's allowance. Everything they carry is small and very well made. They stand still in wind rather than bracing against it, which reads elsewhere as arrogance and is simply what works, and they go quiet and stop making sense in any warm room without once mentioning that they are overheating.

**Names.** Given name, then the rating — the layer number your body is passed for. It is on every document on the world and it is said with the name, so a name is a name and an altitude. Canopy ratings run high and the Ilmeth give theirs readily. The rating is a breathing certificate and it is also a class marker, and Vantage is unfair about this and has an excellent excuse. Nobody sneers. They ask whether you have considered how long staging takes, and the asking does the work. Teva Seventy-Two. Mirrin Eighty-One. Odo Fifty-Nine. Sevaine Seventy-Seven. Ansel Sixty-Eight. Pell Eighty-Eight.

#### The Kovrasi

**Mixed and enormous — the roofed city holds hundreds of peoples and the Kovrasi are the largest. Deep-sighted are common here and always have been, and so are felines, in a place where nothing is visible past ten metres and a great deal is audible through a wall. Neither fact makes anybody Kovrasi, and both get assumed on first meeting, which Kovrasi find tiring. The shared body-fact is duller: no Kovrasi has ever been rained on from above, and the first time is remembered for life.** · Naught to Forty — the roofed city, and four kilometres of it

*Seed: The Maghrebi and Levantine urban law of the darb: a dead-end lane is owned jointly by the doors that open onto it, with heritable easements of passage, light and drainage that no holder may alter without the consent of every other holder. Vantage stands it on end. The shared thing is a shaft of light, air and falling water two kilometres deep with nine thousand doors on it, and the consent runs the whole height.*

A drop is a light shaft and everyone whose door opens onto it. The doors hold it jointly — the light, the air, the drainage, the traffic, the rain that comes down it out of the cloud band — and nothing on a drop changes without the consent of every door. Not a new vent, not a wider sill, not a lamp above the agreed weight.

Three thousand years of that has produced an enormous body of law and not one page of it. Consents are spoken, because a spoken consent has witnesses and a written one has only a page. A drop-speaker can recite two centuries of them in order; every adult can do their own lifetime, and failing to is the one real shame there is.

You belong to your door rather than to your parents. A child is placed behind a door and learns whatever is done there, and the drop arranges it, so Kovrasi siblings routinely end up in different trades on different drops and find nothing odd in this at all.

**Look.** Indoor colouring across most of them. Dress is low-contrast and unlit — pattern instead of colour, because colour needs light somebody else paid for. Everybody carries a door-token, a worn disc the size of a thumbnail with a number stamped into it, kept in a pocket and never displayed. Kovrasi look at your hands rather than your face, which is where the light is.

**Names.** The same rating, city numbers — One to Forty, and something like two in five Kovrasi are somewhere in the teens. A Kovrasi asks for the number before the name whenever anything on a drop is being arranged, because it decides which sills you can stand at without a mask, and asks for it in the flat tone of somebody confirming a shoe size. Yenna Eleven. Dasko Thirty-Four. Teodra Six. Marek Twenty-Nine. Hulun Forty. Sahn Two.

#### The Haskin

**Mixed. Rock skins and amphibioids do well at fifty-five degrees and wet and there are a great many of both. Oni are common and have a specific problem: the current at a geothermal tap is a roar and horns conduct. Most attuned Haskin of any species are out of the runs before twenty, which is a body fact with a social consequence and everyone there knows the difference.** · The downward build — Naught to Under-Forty

*Seed: Cornish tin-mining tutwork and the monthly setting: a pare, a small standing partnership, bids in public for the right to work a pitch, and the partnership rather than the miner is the contracting person. Vantage keeps the public bid and the partnership-as-person and changes what is bid for. Not ore raised. Heat removed.*

The crew is the person. A crew is eight to twenty people, carries a name older than anyone in it, and is what bids, holds, owes and is owed. Nobody in the deeps hires an individual.

Runs are set monthly and in public. A crew bids the heat it undertakes to move off a given run for a term, the bids are called and taken in front of everyone, and a crew that overbids leaves a district above it hot — and the district will want to know whose crew it was. Everyone knows everyone's standing and none of it is written down.

You are taken on at about twelve and do every job on the run before you are allowed to be good at one. Debt between crews is cover: you worked our run short-handed, we owe you a turn. It is never money and it does not expire.

The attuned leave. A crew loses one child in nine to the roar, sends them up the shafts, and does not make a tragedy of it.

**Look.** They dress for fifty-five degrees and wet — sleeveless, cropped, everything quick-drying — and go on dressing that way everywhere else, so a Haskin in a cold gallery is visibly and cheerfully underdressed. The mark is a fine pale mottling across the backs of both hands from twenty years of working wet and hot. Rock skins among them carry a deep seam of the same three minerals, which another Haskin reads to the run.

**Names.** Rating first, name after, because the run cares what you are rated for and does not much care who you are. Deep ratings are Under numbers and the Haskin say them without decoration and without apology, and are the only people on the world who have never once heard their own number softened. Under-Nine Erwa. Under-Twenty-Two Nesta. Under-Four Bodrun. Under-Thirty-One Ovva. Under-Sixteen Idrek. Under-Two Tal.

#### The Sekkeri

**The most mixed people on Vantage by a distance, because a household takes in whoever it can run the schedule for. Tesk, oni, amphibioid, human, Made, rock skin, all in one household and all breathing the same way. Their one shared advantage is not a body at all — it is fifteen years of being moved, which works on any body that survives it and works on none of them comfortably.** · The fourteen through-shafts, and the staging galleries inside the load decks

*Seed: Berber and Tuareg zettata: safe passage across a territory is guaranteed by the people whose ground it is, with their own property standing surety for anything lost in their section. Vantage turns the territory on its side. A section is four hundred vertical metres, the losses are cargo and pressure and people, and the surety is unlimited.*

A Sekkeri household holds a section — four hundred vertical metres of one through-shaft — and when its turn comes it stands surety for everything moving through. Unlimited, out of its own holdings. A bad turn can finish a household, so the shaft rebuilds it immediately and without discussion, because otherwise nobody could afford to take a turn at all.

The turn cycle runs years, because acclimatisation does, and that is the other half of the arrangement. Sekkeri children are staged: fifteen years of being moved up and down on a pattern the household holds, ending in an adult who can work the whole column, deep runs to top masts, which nobody else on Vantage can do at all. It is upbringing rather than body, and a household will take in anyone it can run the schedule for. Most of them have.

The cost is fifteen years of being slightly ill on purpose, and never having the same neighbours twice.

**Look.** Nothing in the body, which is the point. What you see is the bottle — a small shoulder-slung pressure flask carried at every level whether the level needs one or not, because a Sekkeri without one has forgotten something — and the breathing, four in and six out, which starts the moment any floor begins to move and which none of them know they are doing.

**Names.** A range instead of a number, because the staging gave them the whole column. It is unwieldy, it is said in full on documents, and among themselves they give the top figure only. Everyone else hears a range and does not know where to put them, and the Sekkeri have quietly understood for nine hundred years that this is the most valuable thing they own. Ivrit Four-to-Eighty. Casun Naught-to-Seventy-One. Hal Six-to-Eighty-Eight. Meddrun Under-Twelve-to-Sixty. Torrey Under-Thirty-to-Fifty-Two. Ossenne Under-Five-to-Ninety.

### How they regard each other

**The Ilmeth and the Kovrasi** — *getting worse, quickly, and neither side is doing anything wrong*

Sixty years ago collector film changed. Modern film is a ninth the mass at twice the yield, so a mast re-stayed to current practice can be shorter and sit further inboard — which moves it off the drop its charge requires it to light. An Ilmeth cannot repurpose a charge, and exchange needs a structure of exactly equal standing mass, and there is none spare. A Kovrasi cannot consent to a darkening, because consent is unanimous and a drop has nine thousand doors. For three thousand years this came up perhaps once a decade. It now comes up eleven times a year, because the masts in question were all stood inside the same forty-year burst after the Fall and are all coming due together. Each side's own law is the thing stopping it.

**The Haskin, the Kovrasi and the Sekkeri** — *three-sided by construction — no pair is the unit*

Heat comes off the bottom, air and light come down the drops, and everything and everybody moves through fourteen shafts. Any two of the three can agree something and it does not hold, because the third's number is inside it: a crew's heat bid depends on the water the doors send down, the doors' drainage depends on which shafts are running, and a shaft's turn depends on what the deeps are venting that month. So the monthly setting has been a three-way argument in public for eleven hundred years and none of the three has ever thought of the other two as a pair. They marry across all three without anyone finding it worth remarking on, and they are collectively baffled by the canopy business, and have twice offered to sort it out, unhelpfully.

**The Ilmeth and the Haskin** — *mutual non-registration*

Twelve kilometres and two load decks apart, and nothing between them at all. No trade: the canopy wants nothing the deeps make, and what the deeps move is heat, which does not go up — it goes sideways into the roofed seas. No people either, because a deep-bred twelve-year-old would need three years of staging to stand upright in the canopy, and nobody has ever thought that was worth it. There is no dispute because there has never been a transaction. Both peoples hold, sincerely and in almost the same words, that they are the ones holding the world up. Neither has ever heard the other's version, so the claim has never been contested by anyone. Told about each other, both are polite and slightly bored.

**The Sekkeri and the other three** — *claimed by everybody, corrects nobody*

Each of the other three has quietly filed the Sekkeri as a variety of itself. The Kovrasi treat a household as a drop stood on its end and ask after its doors. The Haskin file it as an odd sort of crew and include it in cover. The Ilmeth regard a shaft as a peculiar narrow reach and have twice tried to stand a charge on one. Even the range-name gets read three ways: as a door that moves, as a rating with no run attached, as a mass that will not settle. The Sekkeri have never corrected any of it, partly because all three are useful and partly because after nine hundred years it would be rude. What nobody says out loud is that the Sekkeri are the only people on Vantage who have met everybody.

### History

Nobody decided to roof Vantage. They never stopped, the last open ground went under about three thousand years ago, and there is no record of anyone at the time noticing.

Upward for fifteen hundred years after that. Then the Hennet Fall: eleven hundred years ago a load deck beneath the Hennet districts failed and put nine kilometres of stacked building onto the old surface. The figure usually given is four million. The crews who dug it out said more and were not argued with.

The present world is entirely the Fall's doing. No more building up without foundation, so they went down — which is where the heat and the water were, and why the Haskin are a people only eleven hundred years old and behave like it. The load-deck doctrine, holding that a penetration is a debt against the structure payable in compensating mass, which is why forty-three billion people share fourteen through-shafts. The Sekkeri, who came out of the crews that sank those shafts and never went home. And the rating, which began as a medical certificate issued to shaft workers and was on ordinary documents within two generations and inside names within four.

The canopy came last, on the new foundations. The first charges were stood six hundred years ago. The newest mast is forty, and the people who rigged it are alive and extremely pleased about it.

The Census lists the place as Vantage III. Nobody there has ever used the numeral.

### The Null

The Null has not come near Vantage, and nobody there considers it a subject — which on a world of forty-three billion is its own kind of answer. Every hull within reach, working together, could lift a district. So the settings run monthly, the masts get re-stayed, the drops stay lit, and the population figure everyone quotes still comes from the Haskin, who worked it out from water throughput and have never got over themselves about it.

One thing has changed. Since the Census post opened sixty years ago, ordinary people have been turning up at it asking to have things entered. Not the biology, which is what the post is for. The drop consents. The crew names. The charges and the section liabilities and the forty-one exchanges. All of it held in living memory, none of it written anywhere, all of it precisely the sort of thing that leaves nothing behind. Records has never worked out what to do about this and has never once said no, and there is now a wing of the post that exists entirely because of it.

Eleven Vantage people joined *ARC 017 - Star Singer* at the last call. Nine were Sekkeri.

### Hooks

Things a character from here carries:

- Anyone from Vantage can tell you whether a structure is loaded correctly by listening to it for about ten seconds. Any stratum, any trade, no training — you grow up under forty billion tonnes and you learn what a building sounds like when it is unhappy. On a ship with a grown hull that is either the most useful instinct aboard or a source of constant unnecessary alarm, and which one depends on the week.
- Aboard ARC 017 - Star Singer nobody knows what the numbers mean. A Vantage crewmate gives the rating in the first sentence out of reflex, watches the face, and gets nothing back — no adjustment, no second look, no polite question about staging times. It is the single most restful thing about the ship and not one of them has found a way to say so.
- An Ilmeth knows their own mass to the hundred grams and picks things up to find out what they weigh. Your things. Mid-sentence, without asking. They also catch anything falling, always, without looking, because in the canopy a dropped object is four kilometres of falling and lands on somebody — so they catch things other people have not dropped yet, and apologise, and do it again.
- A Kovrasi will not alter anything shared without finding everyone it touches and asking them. Not a vent, not a lamp, not the position of a bench in a gallery. Somebody rerouting a duct because it was quicker genuinely upsets them, and they cannot explain why at a volume that sounds proportionate, because to them it is not a principle. It is what a shared thing is.
- A Haskin gives the crew before the name and has no useful answer to what do you do, because in the deeps that is not a question asked of one person. It reads as modesty or evasion and is neither. The other tell is on the hands — fine pale mottling across the backs of both, off twenty years wet at fifty-five degrees — which deep-run Haskin spot on each other across a refectory instantly and never mention.
- A Sekkeri moves towards an accident before deciding to. Nine hundred years of standing surety for four hundred vertical metres does that. They are baffled by people calling it brave, and they will walk into something they are plainly not equipped for while still working out whose it was.

### Comedy

Kovrasi hospitality ends in a parting gift, and the better the visit went the heavier it is. Refusing cannot be done. So there are keeper-houses in the canopy with four generations of city kettles, stone jars and one brass weight of no discernible purpose stacked at the back, eating allowance a child could have been fed on, and nobody at either end has ever found a way to raise it. An Ilmeth coming home from a good visit below can be identified at a kilometre by the walk.

And a Haskin will tell you where the heat in a room is going. Unprompted, warmly, as an opening — the bodies, the lamps, the hot pipe behind that wall, the door somebody keeps propping. In the deeps this is conversation. Everywhere else it ends conversation, and the Haskin's reading of that is that you must be tired.
