# The world

**Genre: mistpunk.** Solarpunk, but temperate rainforest rather than Mediterranean — the Olympic
Peninsula rather than a rooftop garden. Everything is wet, green, mossy and quiet. Light arrives
filtered, sound arrives muffled, and there is always fog, and the fog is part of how things work.

This is a universe where life is *abundant* — not rare, not precious, not clinging on. It is
everywhere, it is loud, and most of it has never been looked at properly. That is the premise the
whole game rests on: there is more life out there than anyone can possibly record, and something is
erasing it faster than the survey can work.

---

## Mana

**Mana** is the substance. It is what everything is made of, and the six elements — order, chaos,
growth, decay, energy, void — are the forms it takes. **Ley lines** are the currents it runs along:
rivers through the deep, stronger in some places than others, shifting as you travel.

It is completely **ordinary**. Nothing here is arcane, secret or forbidden. Fixing a broken relay
and rematrixing a thermomaga array for better ley harmonies are the same size of job, done on the
same shift, by someone who would be equally bored describing either.

**Mana is heard, not seen.** Everyone who perceives it describes it in the language of sound —
harmony, pitch, beat, a thing being *in tune* with where it sits. A system running well is
harmonised to the local current. Move the ship into a new lane and everything aboard needs retuning,
which is most of what maintenance actually is.

### Hearing it

Some people hear mana and some do not, and it has nothing whatever to do with intelligence,
training or worth.

**The attuned** hear it directly. They tune by ear, feel a structure's harmony with their hands, and
mostly cannot explain what they are doing.

**The deaf** hear nothing, ever, and no amount of practice changes it. Whole worlds are deaf —
**Earth** is the famous one — and the shipboard shorthand is simply *"they hear it"* or *"they
don't,"* asked as casually as handedness.

### What the deaf built

Unable to perceive mana, the deaf worlds spent centuries mastering everything else — mechanics,
electronics, metallurgy, mathematics, programming — and then turned all of it on the problem. They
built instruments that could hear what they could not. Then instruments that could measure it. Then
machines that could *shape* it.

A **thermomaga array** renders raw ley current as heat, light and power, identically every time, on
a dial anyone can read. **Transmana manifolds** distribute it through the ship, and their
characteristic failure is **over-excitation** — a manifold picking up more from a strong current
than its lattice was tuned for. **Rematrixing** is the routine job of retuning a lattice to a new
ley environment, and it is fiddly, unglamorous and constant.

Both traditions work, completely:

| | **Attuned** | **Instrumented** |
|---|---|---|
| How | by ear, by feel | by instrument, by machine |
| Best at | subtlety, novelty, things nobody has catalogued | precision, repeatability, scale |
| Worst at | explaining, teaching, consistency | anything the instrument was not built for |
| Fails by | having an off day | being out of calibration |

An attuned engineer can harmonise a reef nobody has ever surveyed, by ear, in an afternoon, and
cannot write down how. An instrumented crew can bring four hundred identical manifolds to tolerance
and get the same answer at the end of a double shift as at the start. The ship needs both and has
both, and which counts as *properly* engineering is the oldest and friendliest argument aboard.

## The ship

The ***Understory*** is an **arcology** — a self-contained city, built as one structure, that also
happens to travel faster than light.

She is not alive. This matters and people get it wrong: she has no mind, wants nothing, and is not a
creature. What she has is scale, age, and an enormous number of half-understood systems that respond
to their surroundings, which produces behaviour nobody designed and everybody reads as personality.
Passages thicken. Fruit turns up in a gallery that is not agricultural. Quarters get colonised by a
fungus that hums. Nobody seriously thinks she is alive. Everybody talks to her anyway.

### Built frame, grown fabric

**The frame is manufactured and the fabric is grown**, and the interaction of the two is the ship.

An inorganic spaceframe carries the geometry, the drive and the loads. It does not change. Over and
through it grows living stone and structural heartwood, which take vacuum, radiation and cold the
way a cedar takes winter, and which close their own wounds given time and encouragement. A trellis
with two centuries of vine on it.

The understory of a rainforest is the crowded layer beneath the canopy where most of the actual life
is — a good name for a ship that exists to catalogue what nobody has bothered with. The crew call
her **Story**.

### Inside her it rains

Thermal management is a watercourse. Coolant runs as rivers down her length, over falls, through
pools and moss beds and root tangles, and it is clean enough to drink and everyone does. That is
arcology thinking rather than a quirk — density lets one system do several jobs, so the thermal
loop, the water supply and the humidity that keeps the grown structure healthy are all the same
loop. The permanent mist is the byproduct of the whole thing working.

Corridors are galleries between root buttresses. Light comes through leaf canopies that double as
her collectors. Half the surfaces are moss and nobody removes it, because the moss is doing
something.

### Scale

She dwarfs small moons. Her population runs to the millions, and that population is a *city*: public
concourses, markets, gardens, refectories, lecture halls, performance spaces — and private quarters,
labs, workshops and residential halls behind them. There are people three sections over you will
never meet. There are people born aboard who have never set foot on a planet.

## The Long Census, and the university

The institution is a **university**, and the ship is its campus.

Everything aboard is arranged for study. The Census — the great project of cataloguing life before
the Null reaches it — is the university's work, its curriculum and its reason for moving. A survey
is somebody's fieldwork. The catalogue is where you publish, and getting an entry in is credit that
follows your name.

### What this society admires

**Achievement, not celebrity.** People here look up to those who have *done* something — solved it,
built it, found it, kept it running. Sport exists and is enjoyed and nobody is idolised for it. Arts
and performance exist and are valued, and a good singer is a genuine public figure, but for the
singing rather than the fame.

**No work is looked down on**, and this is worth being explicit about because it is easy to get
wrong. The person crawling a duct to fix an over-excited transmana manifold holds a master's in
mana systems. The people who keep the loops closed are among the most qualified aboard, and on an
arcology their work *is* the advanced science — a city that fails its water cycle is a city that
dies. Engineering is a faculty like any other, with its own degrees, its own senior figures and its
own seat on the council.

Rich and poor live in the same halls. It is not a utopia. People are unfair, petty, ambitious and
occasionally awful to each other. It is simply not a place where your job decides your worth.

### Education is not age-gated

You advance when you are ready, and readiness is assessed rather than assumed. A ten-year-old with a
doctorate is unusual but not scandalous. A sixteen-year-old holding a real engineering post is
ordinary. And someone who has failed the same examinations eight years running is still enrolled,
still working, and nobody has suggested they stop — because there is no "you have had your chance"
here. There is only whether you are ready yet.

### The council

Leadership is a **council of department heads**, and it is broadly democratic. Every seat was earned
by doing the work: you lead a faculty because your faculty would not be led by anyone else.

Which produces the standing problem — the best practitioner is now doing politics, and those are
different skills. Several of them are visibly bad at it and got there anyway.

They have real rivalries, mostly about resources: survey time, power allocation, berths, capacity in
the catalogue. The oldest tension is between the faculties that generate prestige and the ones that
keep everybody breathing, and it is a real argument with a real case on both sides.

You meet them gradually, and different people aboard have entirely different relationships with
them.

## The faculties

What actually gets staffed, and where a job title comes from.

| Faculty | Covers |
|---|---|
| **Life Systems** | the closed loops — atmosphere, water, nutrient, waste, cultivation, the groves and herds |
| **Structures** | the built frame and the grown fabric; hull, load, growth, repair |
| **Mana Systems** | thermomaga arrays, transmana manifolds, distribution, harmonics, rematrixing |
| **Propulsion & Navigation** | the drive, helm, and the lanes |
| **Instruments & Robotics** | the machines, sensors and automata — much of it built so the mana-deaf can work |
| **Survey** | the mission: xenobiology, xenochemistry, xenobotany, reef survey, field operations |
| **Records** | the catalogue, the archive, publication |
| **Medicine** | crew and non-crew, of which there are many kinds |
| **Civic** | teaching, food, performance, sport, dispute |

Titles are professional and plain — *hull systems engineer*, *lead, xenobiochemistry*, *catalogue
registrar*, *materials assay*. Grades run technician, engineer, senior, principal, head; the
academic ladder runs alongside it and the two are treated as equivalent.

## Travel: the song lanes

Faster-than-light is solved and unremarkable. What is not solved is that open deep is rough,
unpredictable and slow to cross safely.

The **song lanes** are the answer. Space whales migrate, and their song persists along the routes
they take — and those routes are ley currents, which is what the whales are singing along. Where the
song runs, passage is calm and known. Nebula jellyfish drift the same lanes in enormous slow blooms
and leave fainter traces of their own.

So navigation is **listening**, and a navigator is someone who can hold a song in their head for
forty years and hear the day it changes.

**A lane can go quiet.** When the whales stop using a route — because what it went to is gone — the
song thins, then stops, and the lane closes. A fading lane is the earliest warning anyone gets that
the Null has taken something upstream.

The *Understory* has lost eleven lanes in eleven years.

## Where you survey: the reefs

Asteroid reefs. Rock and ice colonised until the stone is more organism than mineral — layered,
branching, dense with things that eat light, things that eat each other, and things nobody has
described because nobody has been this far out with time to spare.

A reef is a stack of habitats: outer crust, branching middle, and interior voids holding their own
atmospheres and entirely separate ecologies. Most of the work is getting *in* without destroying
what is inside, which is a hard problem and the reason there is a whole discipline of access
engineering.

Some reef life is enormous. Some of it objects to being catalogued. That is where the fighting comes
from — not war, never war. Fieldwork with teeth.

## The Null

Something is unmaking the universe and nobody knows what it is. It was first seen three galaxies
over.

It is not a fleet, an army or a weapon. It arrives, and afterwards there is no wreckage, no ruin, no
remains, no evidence anything was ever there. Not destruction — *absence*, and people who look at it
too long lose the ability to describe what they are looking at.

**Here is why this crew in particular find it obscene.** These are naturalists. They understand rot
better than anyone alive. In a forest, death is the engine — a fallen trunk becomes a nurse log and
the next century of growth stands on top of it. Nothing is wasted or lost, only moved. Decay is
*generous*.

The Null is decay that gives nothing back. There is no soil afterwards and nothing grows in what it
leaves, because it does not leave anything. To people who built their lives on the idea that nothing
is ever truly lost, it is not merely frightening. It is a violation of the one thing they were sure
of.

The Census's official position is that the Null is a phenomenon under study. The Census's official
position is also that the *Understory* is not running from it.

## Why the catalogue matters

Because it is the only copy.

A species the *Understory* does not reach in time does not die — dying leaves something behind. It
is never recorded, and afterwards there is no fossil, no bone, no story and no trace, and as far as
anything that comes later is concerned it simply never happened.

The catalogue is a living archive, a grown seed-vault, a coral of record — the largest single
organism aboard.

This is the weight everyone carries and almost nobody mentions. They argue about taxonomy instead. A
fifteen-year dispute over whether a thing with two mouths counts as one organism is easier to hold
in your head than the other thing, and it is, in its way, exactly the same argument.

## Peoples and worlds

Life is abundant, so people are too. Where someone is from shows — in colouring, in markings, in how
they wear their hair, in what they consider normal. Worlds are named and numbered: **Maelus IV**,
**Utora VII**, **Orrowan V**. The numeral is the orbital position and everyone uses it.

### The Chosokee — Maelus IV

Rich tan skin and white hair from childhood — colouring, not age. Fine pale **markings** across the
cheekbones, shoulders and forearms, partly inherited and partly earned: a Chosokee gains new lines
as they take charge of something living, so reading someone's forearms tells you what they have
responsibility for. Maelus IV is a world of enormous slow fauna and people who have lived alongside
it long enough to be extremely good at it.

They use single names. A surname would strike them as an odd thing to need.

### The Utorans — Utora VII

Deep brown to near-black skin, and **braided hair in patterns that mean something** — family on one
side, discipline on the other, rebraided when either changes. An Utoran scholar's head is a readable
record, which they find practical and everyone else finds slightly intimidating. A wet, forested
world with a long scholarly tradition.

### The Beluni — Belune III

Belune III has the largest tides anywhere surveyed. Twice a day the same ground is a different
place, and the Beluni have never found this remarkable.

Some Beluni are **tidal**: one person, one personality, one life — who shifts between feminine and
masculine on their own schedule. Not two people, not moods, not a transition. The same person, the
same memories and jokes and grudges, presenting differently.

**Their mana changes when they turn**, which is the only reliable tell. Anyone attuned feels it
immediately and finds the whole question trivially easy. The mana-deaf get no signal at all and have
to either ask or guess, and how somebody handles that says a great deal about them.

### The Cassine — Ranohe II

They grow their entire lives and never stop. Skin thickens with age into something like weathered
bark laid down in visible ridges, so a Cassine's arm reads like a cut trunk. Deep-set amber eyes. An
elder is enormous, slow, and eventually cannot move at all, which every Cassine knows and plans
around.

A Cassine takes a new name at each size threshold and each is longer than the last, so the name
tells you roughly how old and how large they are. Using an earlier one is rude.

### The Ossuan — Iseul III

Tall, slender, hairless, pale silver-grey skin, eyes almost entirely pupil — they evolved under a
dim sun and find most ships painfully bright.

**No personal names, only posts.** The post *is* the name. When an Ossuan's function changes their
name changes, and they do not regard the previous holder — who was themselves — as the same person
in any sense worth arguing about.

### Elsewhere

**Kessa** was a ring of inhabited reefs and the best shipwright tradition in reach. The Null took it
four years ago.

Others aboard come from **Verekh III** (orbital yards), **Orrowan V**, **Sindal II**, **Tessine
IX**, **Calyx IV**, **Ombrea V**, and **Tannhul VI**, a high-gravity world that produces people
built like doors.
